    <h1 class="detail-title">From Fitting a Line to Modeling the Noise Behind It</h1>

    <p class="detail-description">
        Ordinary least squares treats fitting a line as a purely geometric/algebraic problem: minimize
        $\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2$, take the derivative, solve. Nothing random involved — it's optimization,
        not statistics.
    </p>

    <p class="detail-description">
        The probabilistic reframing changes the <strong>starting assumption</strong>: instead of just
        having data points, you assume there's a true underlying relationship, and each observed target
        is that true value plus random noise:
    </p>

    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed;">
        $$t_i = wx_i + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$$
    </div>

    <p class="detail-description">
        This is a genuinely different object — a <strong>generative model</strong>. It says: "if you gave
        me the true $w$ and $\sigma^2$, here's the full probability distribution I'd expect to see for
        $t_i$" — specifically $t_i \sim N(wx_i, \sigma^2)$. Once you have that, you can write a
        <strong>likelihood</strong> — the probability of the entire observed dataset, as a function of
        the unknown $w$ — and ask which $w$ makes the data you actually saw most probable. That's MLE.
    </p>

    <h3>Step 1 — What Does "Probability of the Data" Even Mean Here?</h3>
    <p class="detail-description">
        Pick a candidate value, say $w=2$. Under that guess, the <em>predicted</em> values are $2, 4, 6$.
        The <em>actual</em> observed values were $3, 5, 6$. So the noise that "must have happened," under
        this candidate $w$, is: $\epsilon_1=1$, $\epsilon_2=1$, $\epsilon_3=0$.
    </p>
    <p class="detail-description">
        Now — we assumed $\epsilon \sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. That assumption tells us exactly how probable each
        of those specific noise values is. The Gaussian density function gives us a number for "how
        likely is a noise value of exactly 1" versus "how likely is a noise value of exactly 0." That's
        the whole trick: <strong>the noise assumption converts a residual into a probability (density)
        value.</strong>
    </p>

    <h3>Step 2 — Multiply Them Together</h3>
    <p class="detail-description">
        For a given point, the density of observing $y_i$ given $x_i$ and $w$ is:
    </p>
    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed;">
        $$N(y_i; wx_i, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y_i-wx_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$
    </div>
    <p class="detail-description">
        Assuming the three data points are independent, the probability of seeing <em>all three</em>
        together is the product of their individual densities:
    </p>
    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed;">
        $$L(w) = \prod_{i=1}^{3} N(y_i; wx_i,\sigma^2)$$
    </div>
    <p class="detail-description">
        This product, as a function of $w$, is the <strong>likelihood</strong>. Different candidate
        $w$'s give different residuals, which give different density values, which give a different
        overall product. MLE says: find the $w$ that makes this product as large as possible.
    </p>

    <h3>Step 3 — Where the Squared Error Actually Comes From</h3>
    <p class="detail-description">
        Taking the log of that product (logs turn products into sums, and don't change where the maximum
        sits):
    </p>
    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed;">
        $$\log L(w) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(y_i-wx_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]$$
    </div>
    <p class="detail-description">
        The first term inside the brackets doesn't depend on $w$ at all — it's just a constant that shifts
        every candidate $w$'s score equally, so it can't affect <em>which</em> $w$ wins. Strip it out, and
        maximizing what's left is:
    </p>
    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed;">
        $$\max_w \left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i-wx_i)^2\right) \;\equiv\; \min_w \sum_i (y_i-wx_i)^2$$
    </div>
    <p class="detail-description">
        That last step — maximizing a negative thing is the same as minimizing the positive thing inside
        it — is the entire punchline. <strong>The sum-of-squared-residuals term didn't get inserted by
        hand; it fell straight out of the algebra, purely because we chose a Gaussian for the
        noise.</strong> If you'd instead assumed the noise followed a Laplace distribution (density
        $\propto e^{-|\epsilon|/b}$), the exact same three steps would have landed you minimizing
        $\sum_i |y_i - wx_i|$ instead — absolute error, not squared error. The loss function isn't a free
        design choice; it's a <em>consequence</em> of what you assumed about the noise.
    </p>

    <h3>The One Thing MLE Gets That Pure Least Squares Never Asks For</h3>
    <p class="detail-description">
        Notice $\sigma^2$ was sitting there the whole derivation, but least squares never needed it — it
        dropped out of the equation entirely once we started minimizing. MLE, if you also take the
        derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to $\sigma^2$ and set <em>that</em> to zero, hands
        you a second answer for free:
    </p>
    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed;">
        $$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i(y_i - \hat{w}x_i)^2$$
    </div>
    <p class="detail-description">
        — literally the average squared residual, using the best-fit $\hat{w}$ you just found.
        <strong>That's the noise-level estimate least squares has no mechanism to produce, because it
        never had a probabilistic noise model to begin with.</strong>
    </p>

    <div class="intuition-box" style="background-color: #fdf2e9; border-left: 4px solid #e67e22; padding: 15px 20px; margin: 20px 0;">
        <strong>LS vs. MLE, in one line:</strong> LS picks a criterion and directly optimizes it. MLE
        starts one level deeper — with an assumption about how the noise behaves — and that assumption
        happens to hand you the same criterion LS was using all along, plus a noise estimate LS never
        had access to.
    </div>

    <h2>"If MLE Gives the Same $\hat{w}$, Why Bother With It At All?"</h2>
    <p class="detail-description">
        Fair question — and worth asking directly, since we just showed the two methods tie on the fitted
        line itself. The answer is that the probability model behind MLE gives you things pure LS
        structurally cannot, even when the fitted line matches:
    </p>

    <div style="background: #f8f9fa; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed; border-radius: 6px; padding: 18px 22px; margin: 16px 0;">
        <ol style="margin: 0; padding-left: 22px;">
            <li style="margin-bottom: 12px;">
                <strong>It explains <em>why</em> squared error is the right loss.</strong> LS just assumes
                it; MLE derives it from the Gaussian noise assumption. Change that assumption (e.g., to
                Laplace noise) and MLE derives a <em>different</em> loss instead — squared error was
                never a law, just a consequence of one particular noise assumption.
            </li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 12px;">
                <strong>It estimates the noise itself</strong> ($\hat{\sigma}^2$) — needed for confidence
                intervals, hypothesis tests on individual weights, and model-comparison scores (AIC/BIC),
                none of which plain LS can produce.
            </li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 12px;">
                <strong>It gives a full predictive distribution, not just a point.</strong>
                $N(\hat{w}x_{\text{new}}, \hat{\sigma}^2)$ instead of a bare number, so you can say
                "95% of the time the true value falls in this range."
            </li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 12px;">
                <strong>It makes regularization principled instead of ad hoc.</strong> Put a prior on $w$
                (e.g., $w\sim N(0,\tau^2)$) and Ridge regression falls out as the MAP estimate, with
                $\lambda$ meaning something concrete ($\sigma^2/\tau^2$) instead of being an arbitrary
                knob.
            </li>
            <li style="margin-bottom: 0;">
                <strong>It's the exact same machine reused everywhere downstream.</strong> Logistic
                Regression, Gaussian Naive Bayes, GMM/EM, even the SFT cross-entropy loss on the LLM
                Engine page are all "assume a generative model &rarr; write the likelihood &rarr; maximize
                it." Learning MLE here, where it happens to reduce to something familiar, is what makes
                every later derivation feel like reapplication instead of a brand-new trick each time.
            </li>
        </ol>
    </div>
