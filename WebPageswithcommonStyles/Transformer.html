The Transformer Algorithm

A comprehensive architectural breakdown from historical lineage to token-by-token execution.

Sequence-to-Sequence (Seq2Seq) & The Encoder-Decoder Framework

Many real-world language tasks—such as translation, summarization, and question-answering—are sequence-to-sequence (Seq2Seq) problems. They require transforming a variable-length input sequence into a completely different variable-length output sequence.

To handle this, researchers designed the Encoder-Decoder framework, which cleanly splits the work into two dedicated roles:

  • The Encoder: Reads the entire input sequence word-by-word and compresses its complete meaning into a single hidden vector (the "context" vector).
  • The Decoder: Takes this single vector and generates the new output sequence, one token at a time.
💡 The Analogy: Think of the Encoder as a translator who reads and digests a foreign document, and the Decoder as a writer who recreates that document in a new language using only the translator's summary notes -here summary notes means the fixed-length context vector, which can't go back and re-check the source text for a detail it needs.. A complete walkthrough of the Encoder–Decoder Transformer is available here.

From RNNs to Attention: The Engineering Bottleneck

Before the Transformer was introduced in the landmark 2017 paper "Attention Is All You Need", Seq2Seq architectures relied entirely on recurrent networks like RNNs and LSTMs . However, these recurrent systems suffer from massive architectural limitations:

  • The Information Bottleneck: Forcing a 100-word sentence into a single, fixed-length context vector is mathematically inefficient. Important historical details introduced early in a long passage get diluted or completely lost by the time the vector reaches the decoder.
  • Vanishing Gradients: As text sequences grow, backpropagating errors across deep temporal paths causes gradients to vanish or explode, making long-range dependencies incredibly difficult to model.
  • The Parallelization Wall: By definition, RNNs process data sequentially—they cannot compute step $t$ until step $t-1$ is finished. This sequential constraint means they cannot exploit the massive parallel computing power of modern hardware (GPUs), leading to painfully slow training cycles.

While LSTMs mitigated the vanishing gradient problem slightly, their added cell complexity made them even slower to train.

The Solution: Transformers

The algorithm Transformer eliminates recurrence entirely. By replacing sequential loops with Self-Attention mechanisms, it allows the network to look at an entire sequence simultaneously. This structural shift resolves the information bottleneck, captures long-range context perfectly, and allows training to be fully parallelized across GPUs.

The Core Idea in One Line

Instead of reading word by word → Look at ALL words at once and learn relationships between them.

Transformer Architecture

The Transformer model is solely based on attention mechanisms without any convolutional or recurrent layer (Vaswani et al., 2017). Though originally proposed for sequence-to-sequence learning on text data, Transformers have been pervasive in a wide range of modern deep learning applications, such as in areas to do with language, vision, speech, and reinforcement learning. The Transformer is a deep neural network architecture that can process variable-length text and perform a wide range of NLP tasks using a mechanism called Attention, discussed below.

Input Preparation: Tokenization, Embeddings, and Positional Encoding

Before raw text can be processed by neural networks, it must undergo a foundational pipeline that converts human-readable strings into structured numerical vectors.

1. Tokenization (Slicing the Text)

Neural networks cannot process raw strings directly; text must be sliced into smaller foundational units called tokens. Modern architectures (like Byte-Pair Encoding or WordPiece sub-word tokenizers) break rare words down into sub-word components to handle out-of-vocabulary terms efficiently, while keeping common whole words as single tokens.

How the vocabulary itself is learned — Byte-Pair Encoding (BPE): the sub-word vocabulary isn't designed by hand; it's learned from a training corpus by repeatedly merging the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols.

BPE Algorithm (Vocabulary Construction)
  1. Start with a base vocabulary of every individual character (or byte) in the corpus.
  2. Represent each word as a sequence of these characters, plus an end-of-word marker.
  3. Count the frequency of every adjacent symbol pair $(a, b)$ across the corpus, and identify the most frequent one: $$ (a^{*}, b^{*}) = \underset{(a,\,b)}{\arg\max}\;\; \text{count}(a, b) $$
  4. Merge $a^{*}$ and $b^{*}$ into a single new symbol $a^{*}b^{*}$, and add it to the vocabulary.
  5. Repeat steps 3–4 for a fixed number of merges (a hyperparameter, typically tens of thousands) or until a target vocabulary size $V$ is reached.

Compact worked example: a toy corpus with low (×5), lower (×2), newest (×6), widest (×3), each first written as characters plus an end-of-word marker · (l o w ·, l o w e r ·, n e w e s t ·, w i d e s t ·). The most frequent pair is (e, s) — appearing $6+3=9$ times across newest and widest — so e+s merges first into es. The next most frequent pair, (es, t), also occurs $9$ times and merges into est. That suffix is now its own reusable token, shared by both words (and any future word like fastest, never seen during training) — this is the core mechanism that lets sub-word tokenization generalize beyond its training vocabulary.

Why Start From Characters, Not Whole Words?

This seems backwards at first — why build up from single letters instead of just starting with the words already sitting in the corpus? Because starting from whole words would just recreate the exact problem this section opened with: a whole-word vocabulary can never cover every word that might show up later — a typo, a rare name, a brand-new slang term — so anything missing has no token at all. And there's no natural stopping point either: new words keep getting coined, so a "complete" word-level vocabulary can never really be finished.

Characters (or bytes) don't have that problem — they're a small, closed set that can be fully listed on day one. English has ~26 letters plus punctuation and digits; byte-level tokenizers cap out at exactly 256 possible values. Nothing can ever fall outside that set, because any string, in any language, with any typo, is still just a sequence of characters. So the algorithm's real strategy is: start from the level that's guaranteed to cover everything, then earn your way up to bigger, more useful units through the merge steps — and if something genuinely novel ever shows up (like flibbertigibbet), the tokenizer can still fall all the way back to spelling it out letter by letter instead of hitting a dead end.

A couple of examples make this concrete:

  • A brand-new word: Suppose "chatbotting" never appeared in the training corpus. A whole-word tokenizer has no token for it — dead end. A character-based BPE tokenizer can still spell it out via whatever merges it does have, e.g. ["chat", "bot", "t", "ing"] — degraded, but never broken.
  • A typo: "recieve" (misspelled) has no whole-word token either. Character-level BPE just falls back further, e.g. ["rec", "iev", "e"] — clunky, but still fully representable, one character at a time if it has to.

Applying the learned vocabulary to new text:

  • Input Sentence: "Transformers are brilliant!"
  • Tokenized Array: ["Transform", "ers", " are", " brilliant", "!"]
  • Mapped Integer IDs: [7942, 2108, 389, 8642, 0]

This creates a genuine trade-off: a larger vocabulary $V$ gives shorter token sequences for the same text (good for compute and context length), but also enlarges the embedding table below and gives rarer tokens less training signal each. Production vocabularies typically run ~30K–50K tokens (GPT-2, BERT) up to 100K+ for the largest current models.

2. Embeddings (Translating Text to Vectors)

Once tokens are mapped to integer IDs, they pass through an Embedding Layer (a lookup table of learned parameters). This algorithm projects each discrete token ID into a continuous high-dimensional vector space of size $d_{\text{model}}$ (e.g., 512 or 4096 dimensions) — a space where the rest of the network can do ordinary linear algebra instead of operating on raw integers.

Basic Algorithm & Steps:

  1. Initialize a weight matrix $W_{\text{embed}} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d_{\text{model}}}$, where $V$ is vocabulary size — one row per token, filled with small random values before training begins.
  2. Represent a token index $i$ as a one-hot vector $e_i \in \{0,1\}^{V}$: all zeros except a single $1$ at position $i$.
  3. Look up the embedding by projecting the one-hot vector through the weight matrix: $$ v_i = e_i^{T} W_{\text{embed}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}} $$ Because $e_i$ only selects one row, this is mathematically a matrix multiply but computationally just a direct row lookup, $v_i = W_{\text{embed}}[i]$ — no multiplication is actually carried out in practice.
  4. Stack the embeddings for every token in the sequence, in order, into a matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}}$ ($n$ = sequence length). This is exactly the $X$ matrix used to seed $Q, K, V$ in the Self-Attention derivation further down this page.
  5. Train $W_{\text{embed}}$ like any other weight matrix, via backpropagation from the training loss. Tokens that play similar roles in similar contexts drift toward similar vectors as training proceeds — the vectors carry no meaning at initialization; they earn it through gradient descent.

"Line up the vectors" means this: if the sentence is "Cat sat mat," Box 3–4 runs three times, once per word, producing three separate vectors — one for "Cat," one for "sat," one for "mat." Box 5 just writes those three vectors down one under the other, like three rows in a table. That table (3 rows here, $n$ rows in general, one per token) is $X$ — nothing is combined or changed, they're simply placed side by side as rows.

What the dashed red "training loop" actually means, step by step: (1) the model uses $X$ to make a guess — e.g. guessing the next word; (2) we compare that guess to the correct answer and get one number describing how wrong it was — that number is the loss; (3) for every single number inside the network, including every number inside $W_{\text{embed}}$, we ask "if this one number were slightly bigger or smaller, would the loss go up or down, and by how much?" — that answer is called a gradient, and computing it for every number at once, working backward from the loss, is called backpropagation; (4) every number then gets nudged a tiny step in whichever direction makes the loss smaller — that's the update. This whole four-step process is what "training" means, and it repeats millions of times. It only happens while the model is being trained — once training is done and the model is just generating text, steps 2–4 never run again; only the solid indigo forward path (Boxes 1–5) keeps running, reading $W_{\text{embed}}$ but never changing it.

One token's ID becomes one row of the embedding table; the same lookup repeats for every token in the sequence, and the results stack into $X$ — the matrix that opens the Self-Attention derivation.

Tiny worked example: a 3-word vocabulary (Cat = id 0, sat = id 1, mat = id 2) with $d_{\text{model}}=4$:

$$W_{\text{embed}} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.8 & 0.1 & 0.5 \\ 0.9 & 0.1 & 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 & 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{id 0: "Cat"} \\ \leftarrow \text{id 1: "sat"} \\ \leftarrow \text{id 2: "mat"} \end{matrix}$$

Tokenizing "Cat sat mat" gives IDs [0, 1, 2]; looking each up and stacking the rows gives exactly the $X$ matrix that opens the Self-Attention Mathematical Derivation below — this step is where that matrix actually comes from. With a realistic vocabulary of $V=50{,}000$ and $d_{\text{model}}=768$, this one table already holds $V \times d_{\text{model}} \approx 38.4$ million parameters, before a single Transformer layer is added. Many production models tie this same matrix to the output layer that projects back to vocabulary-sized logits, to avoid paying that parameter cost twice.

Both tokenization and embedding output numbers, but they serve entirely different roles in an AI pipeline. Tokenization converts text into a sequence of simple integer IDs, meaning its output size fluctuates based on the length of your input text. For example, the word "I" will always receive the exact same static ID, like 10, regardless of the sentence it appears in. Embedding then takes those IDs and transforms each one into a fixed-length list of decimals, called a vector, whose size is hardcoded by the model's architecture (such as 768 or 1536 dimensions). In modern transformer models, these embedding numbers dynamically shift to capture context, meaning the ID 10 for "I" will generate a different final vector in "I bank at Chase" than in "I sit on the river bank"

3. Positional Encoding (Adding Order Information)

Because Transformers process all tokens simultaneously (losing the sequential order inherent to RNNs), they have no built-in awareness of word positions. To fix this, Positional Encodings are injected directly into the input embeddings by adding deterministic sinusoidal signals:

$$PE_{(pos, 2i)} = \sin\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)$$ $$PE_{(pos, 2i+1)} = \cos\left(\frac{pos}{10000^{2i/d_{\text{model}}}}\right)$$

Self-Attention: Context-Aware Weighting

Consider this text example parsed by the architecture:
"The cat sat on the mat because it was soft"

When processing the pronoun "it", the attention mechanism distributes dynamic scalar scores across the contextual tokens. These softmax-normalized weights are optimized during training:

  • it → cat : 0.05 (low contextual relationship)
  • it → mat : 0.85  ← Resolved Target (High semantic relationship because "it" refers to "mat")
  • it → sat : 0.10 (low contextual relationship)

Self-Attention Mathematical Derivation ($Q, K, V$)

The Query, Key, and Value Intuition:

Every token projection matrix queries the surrounding space by formulating three operational vector profiles:

  • $Q$ (Query): "What descriptive properties am I searching for?"
  • $K$ (Key): "What semantic identities and profiles do I contain?"
  • $V$ (Value): "What actual informational payload do I yield or pass forward?"

Step-by-Step Mathematical Flow

Step 1: Input Vector Matrix Construction ($X$)

Given a baseline sentence layout: "Cat sat mat", each word is mapped to a static embedding space ($d_{\text{model}} = 4$):

$$X = \begin{bmatrix} 0.2 & 0.8 & 0.1 & 0.5 \\ 0.9 & 0.1 & 0.7 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & 0.6 & 0.2 & 0.8 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{Cat} \\ \leftarrow \text{sat} \\ \leftarrow \text{mat} \end{matrix}$$

Step 2: Linear Projection using Learned Weight Parameters

The shared input matrix $X$ undergoes projection via specialized learned transformation weights $W^Q, W^K, W^V$ to initialize the operational matrices:

$$Q = X \times W^Q \quad \lvert \quad K = X \times W^K \quad \lvert \quad V = X \times W^V$$

Step 3: Calculating Scaled Dot-Product Attention Scores

Pairwise token alignment is computed by multiplying the Queries matrix directly by the transposed Keys matrix ($Q \times K^T$). This is scaled by the square root of the key dimension ($\sqrt{d_k}$) to prevent extreme variance bounds in deep networks:

$$\text{Score Matrix} = \frac{Q \times K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1.0 & 0.2 & 0.1 \\ 0.3 & 1.0 & 0.2 \\ 0.1 & 0.2 & 1.0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Step 4: Softmax Normalization

We apply a row-wise Softmax operation to transform these raw scores into positive probability values that sum to 1.0:

$$\text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q \times K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) = \begin{bmatrix} 0.70 & 0.20 & 0.10 \\ 0.25 & 0.65 & 0.10 \\ 0.10 & 0.15 & 0.75 \end{bmatrix} \begin{matrix} \leftarrow \text{Cat attends 70\% to itself, 20\% to sat, 10\% to mat} \\ \leftarrow \text{sat attends 25\% to Cat, 65\% to itself, 10\% to mat} \\ \leftarrow \text{mat attends 10\% to Cat, 15\% to sat, 75\% to itself} \end{matrix}$$

Step 5: Aggregating the Final Value Vectors Matrix

The normalized attention coefficient weights filter the Value matrix ($V$) via matrix multiplication, outputting a context-enriched token map:

$$\text{Output} = \text{Softmax}\left(\frac{Q \times K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right) \times V$$

The Universal Formulation

$$\text{Attention}(Q,K,V) = \text{Softmax}\left( \frac{Q \times K^T}{\sqrt{d_k}} \right) \times V$$

Multi-Head Attention Optimization

Rather than tracking relationships through a single attention mechanism, the model parallelizes the operation across $H$ distinct "heads." Each head maintains a completely separate set of projection weights ($W^Q_i, W^K_i, W^V_i$). This lets the model focus on different aspects of the text simultaneously:

  • Head 1: Tracks core syntax and grammatical structures.
  • Head 2: Maps long-range semantic coreferences and pronouns.
  • Head 3: Evaluates immediate spatial or positional tokens.
$$\text{MultiHead}(Q, K, V) = \text{Concat}(\text{Head}_1, \text{Head}_2, \dots, \text{Head}_H) \times W^O$$

What Does the Multi-Head Attention Output Look Like?

The key fact: Multi-Head Attention's final output has exactly the same shape as the input $X$ it started from — $n \times d_{\text{model}}$ (one vector per token, same dimensionality as before). Here's how it gets there:

  1. Each head works in a smaller slice. With $H$ heads, each head doesn't get the full $d_{\text{model}}$ dimensions — it works in $d_k = d_{\text{model}}/H$. Each head has its own $W^Q_i, W^K_i, W^V_i$ projecting down to size $d_k$, and produces its own attention output of shape $n \times d_k$.
  2. Concatenate the heads side by side along the feature dimension: $$ \text{Concat}(\text{Head}_1, \dots, \text{Head}_H) \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (H \cdot d_k)} = \mathbb{R}^{n \times d_{\text{model}}} $$ Since $H \times d_k = d_{\text{model}}$ by construction, this lands right back at the original width — split into $H$ parallel "views," then glued back together.
  3. Mix the heads together with $W^O$. Right now each chunk of the concatenated vector is still "owned" by one head — nothing has combined what Head 1 learned with what Head 2 learned. The final projection $W^O \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{\text{model}}}$ does that mixing, producing the final output — still $n \times d_{\text{model}}$.

Concretely, extending the "Cat sat mat" example ($d_{\text{model}}=4$, $n=3$): with $H=2$ heads, each head produces a $3\times2$ output; concatenating gives $3\times4$; multiplying by $W^O$ ($4\times4$) gives a final $3\times4$ output — the same shape as the original $X$ matrix.

Why the Shape Has to Match Exactly The very next step is the residual connection — $\text{Output}_{\text{residual}} = X + \text{Attention}(X)$ — which is just element-wise addition. That's only possible if $\text{Attention}(X)$ comes out the same shape as $X$. So the concat-then-project design isn't arbitrary — it's what guarantees attention's output can be added back to its own input before heading into Add & Norm, then the Feed-Forward Network.

Feed-Forward Networks: Processing Each Token Independently

Definition: Self-attention is where tokens "talk" to each other — each token's vector is rebuilt as a weighted mix of every other token's Value vector. The Feed-Forward Network (FFN) is the opposite: a small two-layer neural network applied independently and identically to every token position, with zero interaction between positions. If attention is where information moves between tokens, the FFN is where each token's own representation gets extra non-linear processing capacity.

Feed-Forward Network Algorithm (Applied per Token)
  1. Take a single token's vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ — the output of self-attention, after the residual connection and layer normalization.
  2. Expand: project up into a wider hidden space, $h = \text{Activation}(xW_1 + b_1)$, where $W_1 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times d_{ff}}$. The inner dimension $d_{ff}$ is typically 4× $d_{\text{model}}$ (e.g. $d_{ff}=3072$ when $d_{\text{model}}=768$).
  3. Apply a non-linearity — ReLU in the original Transformer, GELU in most modern architectures — so the network can represent more than a linear transformation.
  4. Project back down to the original size: $y = hW_2 + b_2$, where $W_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{ff} \times d_{\text{model}}}$, restoring shape $d_{\text{model}}$ so the vector can flow into the next layer.
  5. This exact same $W_1, W_2$ pair is reused for every token position in the sequence — hence "position-wise" — but each layer of the Transformer has its own separate FFN weights.
$$\text{FFN}(x) = \text{Activation}(xW_1 + b_1)\,W_2 + b_2$$
Where the Parameters Actually Live It's easy to think of attention ($W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$) as the "main event" of a Transformer, but the FFN typically holds the majority of a layer's parameters. With $d_{ff}=4d_{\text{model}}$, $W_1$ and $W_2$ together contain $2 \times d_{\text{model}} \times d_{ff} = 8\,d_{\text{model}}^2$ parameters per layer — roughly double the $\sim4d_{\text{model}}^2$ used by $W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O$ combined. Most of a large model's total parameter count is FFN weight, not attention weight.

Repeat Stacked Iteratively Across N Layers

Definition: Multi-Head Attention (above) is about width — several attention "views" computed in parallel within one layer. Stacking is about depth — taking the entire block you've just seen (Self-Attention → Add & Norm → FFN → Add & Norm) and repeating it $N$ times in sequence, each repetition with its own separate, independently-learned weights. This is the actual "Transformer" in the architecture diagrams you'll see elsewhere — one Transformer block, stacked $N$ deep.

Layer-Stacking Algorithm
  1. Let $h^{(0)} = X$ — the embeddings plus positional encoding, the same matrix that opened the Self-Attention derivation above.
  2. For each layer $l = 1, 2, \dots, N$, feed the previous layer's output through one full Transformer block: $$ h^{(l)} = \text{TransformerBlock}_l\big(h^{(l-1)}\big) $$ where $\text{TransformerBlock}_l$ is exactly the Self-Attention + FFN pipeline covered above, using layer $l$'s own $W^Q, W^K, W^V, W^O, W_1, W_2$ — no weights are shared between layers.
  3. The output of layer $l$ becomes the input to layer $l+1$; nothing about the sequence length or $d_{\text{model}}$ changes between layers, only the content of the vectors.
  4. After $N$ repetitions, $h^{(N)}$ is the final, deeply context-enriched representation — this is what feeds into Output Projections, covered next.

This diagram is deliberately a level "zoomed out" from the stacking diagram above — the $\times N$ box here is that entire diagram, collapsed to one box, so this view can show what that one doesn't: that Embeddings and Output Projection are trained by the exact same backward pass as every layer inside the stack, not by separate training loops.

The dashed bracket is the classic "×N" notation from the original Transformer diagram: everything inside it is the same block structure, repeated $N$ times — but each repetition trains its own separate weights, so layer 1 and layer 50 are not computing the same function.

What Does Each $h^{(l)}$ Actually Represent?

Every $h^{(l)}$ has the same shape as the input — $n$ vectors, one per token, each still in $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$. What changes across layers isn't the shape; it's how much context each token's vector has absorbed. $h^{(0)}=X$ carries almost no awareness of the other tokens — just a token's identity plus its position. $h^{(1)}$ has mixed in one round of attention: typically fairly local, surface-level patterns (adjacent-word relationships, rough syntax). Each further layer lets information propagate further, since a token can now attend to another token that itself already attended to something several tokens away — so deeper layers tend to pick up progressively longer-range and more abstract relationships, up to $h^{(N)}$, the fully context-enriched representation that Output Projections consumes.

Two things worth being precise about: first, "layer 3 = syntax, layer 8 = semantics" is an empirical tendency seen in probing-classifier research on trained models, not an architectural guarantee — there's no rule forcing any particular layer to learn any particular concept. Second, because every block ends in a residual connection ($h^{(l)} = h^{(l-1)} + \text{Block}(h^{(l-1)})$, as covered in The Mechanics Inside a Single Transformer Layer), each layer doesn't replace the previous representation — it adds an incremental refinement on top of it. So $h^{(l)}$ is best read as "everything token $i$ knew after $l-1$ layers, plus whatever this layer's attention and FFN decided to add" — a running total often called the residual stream in interpretability work.

For a concrete, worked walkthrough of what actually changes in each layer's output (grammar in Layer 1, subject-action pairing in Layer 2, and so on) — see the token-by-token execution map in The Internal Anatomy of a Modern LLM, further down this page. $N$ itself is just another hyperparameter, chosen at design time: 12 for GPT-2, 96 for GPT-3, 80 for LLaMA 3 (see the reference table at the end of this page) — more layers generally means more representational depth, at the direct cost of more parameters and slower inference.

Same shape in, same shape out ($d_{\text{model}}$) — the expansion to $d_{ff}$ and back happens entirely inside the block, giving the network extra non-linear capacity without changing what the next layer receives.

Output Projections: From Vectors Back to Tokens

Definition: After $N$ layers of self-attention + FFN, every token position holds a final, deeply context-enriched vector in $\mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ — but that's still just a vector, not a prediction. The Output Projection (often called the "LM head" or "unembedding layer") converts that vector back into a probability distribution over the vocabulary — the exact reverse operation of the Embeddings step that opened the pipeline.

Output Projection Algorithm
  1. Take the final-layer vector $h_{\text{final}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}}}$ for a token position. During generation, only the last position's vector is needed (to predict the next token); during training, every position's vector is projected in parallel so a loss can be computed everywhere at once.
  2. Project up to vocabulary size with a linear layer: $$ \text{logits} = h_{\text{final}}\,W_{\text{unembed}} + b, \qquad W_{\text{unembed}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times V} $$ This produces one raw score ("logit") per token in the vocabulary.
  3. Apply softmax to turn the logits into a valid probability distribution over the $V$ vocabulary tokens: $$ P(\text{token}=k) = \frac{e^{\text{logit}_k}}{\sum_{j=1}^{V} e^{\text{logit}_j}} $$
  4. Select the next token — greedily (highest-probability token), or by sampling from the distribution (temperature, top-k, or top-p / nucleus sampling, which is what gives generated text controllable randomness).
Closing the Loop with Embeddings Notice the shapes: Embeddings mapped a token id ($V$-dimensional, one-hot) to a vector ($d_{\text{model}}$-dimensional) using $W_{\text{embed}} \in \mathbb{R}^{V \times d_{\text{model}}}$. Output Projection does the reverse — vector back to a $V$-dimensional distribution — using $W_{\text{unembed}} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_{\text{model}} \times V}$. Because these two matrices have exactly transposed shapes, many production models set $W_{\text{unembed}} = W_{\text{embed}}^{T}$ directly — a technique called weight tying — reusing one learned matrix for both directions instead of paying its $\sim V \times d_{\text{model}}$ parameter cost twice.

The Internal Anatomy of a Modern LLM (e.g., Claude)

Large Language Models stack dozens of these individual Transformer layers together (typically 96+ layers in production variants) to build deep, abstract reasoning capabilities.

How Tokens Move Through the Stack Together

A common misconception is that models process text word-by-word through the layers. In reality, all tokens in a sequence travel through all layers together at the exact same time as a unified data matrix.

Let's map out how the sequence "The cat sat on the mat" moves through the network step-by-step to predict the next word ("and"):

[Execution Map: Layer Stack Pass-Through]
Input Tokens: [T1: "The", T2: "cat", T3: "sat", T4: "on", T5: "the", T6: "mat"]
↓ All 6 tokens are converted to dense vectors and stacked into a matrix

Layer 1 Execution (All 6 Vectors Enter Simultaneously):
• Self-Attention: "mat" vector calculates dynamic relationships with "The", "cat", "sat", "on".
• Feed-Forward Network: Processes each vector column independently.
• Output: $T_1', T_2', T_3', T_4', T_5', T_6'$ (Vectors updated with basic grammar context)
↓ Layer 1 outputs feed directly as inputs into Layer 2
Layer 2 Execution (All 6 Refined Vectors Enter):
• Learns subject-action pairings (e.g., matching the action "sat" to the subject "cat").
• Output: $T_1'', T_2'', T_3'', T_4'', T_5'', T_6''$
↓ Sequential layer flow loops through N layers...
Final Layer N Execution:
• Builds a deep semantic map of the entire scene (a calm cat resting on a surface).
• Output: $T_1^N, T_2^N, T_3^N, T_4^N, T_5^N, T_6^N$ (Full context-rich matrix)
The Next-Token Prediction Filter:
To generate the next word, the model checks only the final representation of the last token ($T_6^N$ "mat"). Because of the self-attention mechanism, this vector has already ingested information from all preceding words.

Output Softmax Layer Probability Distribution:
• "and"    → 0.35 (Highest Probability Target → Selected)
• "then"   → 0.20
• "."      → 0.18

Once "and" is generated, it is appended to the input sequence. The new sequence, "The cat sat on the mat and" (now 7 tokens), is passed through all $N$ layers again to predict the next token: "slept". This autoregressive loop repeats until an end-of-sequence token is generated.

The Mechanics Inside a Single Transformer Layer

As vectors travel through the network, the raw mathematical outputs can easily degrade or grow too large across 96+ layers. To keep values stable, each layer runs internal normalization and residual tracking pipelines:

Detailed Matrix Pipeline Between and Inside Layers:
Let's look at the transformations a single vector (like "cat") undergoes within one layer:
  1. Self-Attention: Token vectors interact to capture context.
  2. Residual Connection (Skip Layer): The raw incoming input vector is added back directly to the attention output: $$\text{Output}_{\text{residual}} = X + \text{Attention}(X)$$ Why? This preserves early features and prevents structural information from getting lost in deep networks.
  3. Layer Normalization: Standardizes the values across the vector dimensions to maintain a stable mean of 0 and variance of 1. This prevents numbers from exploding or vanishing.
  4. Feed-Forward Network (FFN): Passes the normalized vector through dense neural network layers for non-linear feature processing (full algorithm and math in the dedicated Feed-Forward Networks section above).
  5. Second Residual & Layer Norm: The FFN input is added back to its output and normalized one more time before entering the next layer.

Complete Training loop of Transformer

The solid slate arrows are one forward pass, run once. The dashed red path is one backward pass — not three separate ones. There is only one loss $L$, computed once, and the chain rule computes $\partial L/\partial\theta$ for every parameter $\theta$ in the network in that single sweep. So it's not "train Embeddings, then train Attention, then train FFN" — every parameter, in every component, is updated together, in the same optimizer step, every single training iteration.

How Large Language Models are Trained

Building production models like Claude requires a three-stage training pipeline:

1. Unsupervised Pre-Training

The model ingests massive datasets (websites, books, code). By predicting the next token billions of times, it naturally learns grammar, facts, world logic, and foundational reasoning patterns.

2. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

The pre-trained model is fine-tuned on high-quality, curated dialog templates. This teaches the system how to format answers and behave like an interactive, helpful AI assistant.

3. RLHF & Constitutional AI

Uses Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback along with explicit safety rules (Constitutional AI). This step aligns the model's outputs with core principles like being helpful, harmless, and honest.
