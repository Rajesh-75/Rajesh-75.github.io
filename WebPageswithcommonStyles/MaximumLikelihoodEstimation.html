Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)

From a purely statistical perspective, Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) is a method used to estimate the unknown parameters of a probability distribution based on observed data.

In frequentist statistics, we assume that the observed data ($X$) comes from a specific type of distribution (like a Normal, Poisson, or Binomial distribution), but we don't know the exact "settings" or parameters ($\theta$) of that distribution. MLE provides a systematic way to find the "best" values for these parameters.

1. The Likelihood Function

The core of MLE is the Likelihood Function, denoted as $L(\theta | X)$. While it looks mathematically similar to a probability density function, the perspective is reversed:

Probability $P(X | \theta)$: "Given these fixed parameters, what is the probability of seeing this data?"

Likelihood $L(\theta | X)$: "Given this fixed data, how plausible are these specific parameter values?". In othewords, MLE chooses the parameters that make the data we actually see as the most likely outcome to have occurred.

Mathematically, if we have a set of independent and identically distributed (iid) observations $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, the joint likelihood is the product of their individual probabilities:

$$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i; \theta)$$

2. The Goal of Maximization

The "Maximum" in MLE refers to the goal: we want to find the specific value of $\theta$ (denoted as $\hat{\theta}$) that maximizes the likelihood function. In plain English, we are looking for the parameter value that makes the data we actually saw the most likely outcome to have occurred.

3. The Use of Log-Likelihood

In practice, statisticians almost always work with the Log-Likelihood, $\ell(\theta) = \log L(\theta)$. This is done for two main reasons:

  • Mathematical Simplification: It turns products (which are hard to differentiate) into sums (which are easy to differentiate).
  • Numerical Stability: Multiplying many small probabilities can lead to "arithmetic underflow" in computers; adding logs prevents this.
$$\log L(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log f(x_i; \theta)$$
Note: Since the logarithm is a "monotonically increasing" function, the value of $\theta$ that maximizes the log-likelihood is exactly the same as the one that maximizes the original likelihood.

4. The Statistical Process

To find the MLE, the process typically involves:

  1. Writing down the likelihood function for the chosen distribution.
  2. Taking the natural log to obtain the log-likelihood.
  3. Calculating the derivative with respect to the parameter(s) $\theta$.
  4. Setting the derivative to zero and solving for $\theta$ to find the peak.

5. Why Statisticians Trust MLE

MLE is favored because it possesses powerful "asymptotic" properties as the sample size increases:

  • Consistency: The estimate $\hat{\theta}$ converges (in probability) to the true value of the parameter. Section 6 explains why.
  • Asymptotic Efficiency: For large samples its variance reaches the Cramér–Rao lower bound, $1/(n\,I(\theta))$, the smallest possible among (asymptotically) unbiased estimators. In small samples the MLE can be biased.
  • Asymptotic Normality: As you collect more data, $\sqrt{n}(\hat{\theta}-\theta_0)$ approaches a Normal distribution, so the distribution of the estimate starts to look like a Normal Distribution.

6. How Does the MLE Converge to the True Parameter?

Section 5 states that $\hat{\theta}$ converges to the true value $\theta_0$. This section explains why that happens, how fast it happens, and where the claim needs care. The idea rests on two facts: the Law of Large Numbers, and the fact that the true parameter is the unique maximiser of the expected log-likelihood.

6.1 Intuition: Repeated Sampling

Consider an opinion poll before a state election. Suppose the true vote share of a party is $p_0 = 0.4$. We poll $n$ voters and record the fraction $\hat{p} = k/n$ who say they will vote for the party. This $\hat{p}$ is the MLE of a Bernoulli parameter.

If we ran the poll again with a fresh random sample, we would get a slightly different $\hat{p}$. So the estimate is itself a random variable, and what matters is how its distribution behaves as $n$ grows. Its spread shrinks like $\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}$:

Voters polled ($n$)Standard error of $\hat{p}$
100$\sqrt{0.24/100}\approx 0.049$ (about 4.9 percentage points)
1,000$\sqrt{0.24/1000}\approx 0.015$ (about 1.5 percentage points)
10,000$\sqrt{0.24/10000}\approx 0.005$ (about 0.5 percentage points)
Note: The error shrinks like $1/\sqrt{n}$, so you need four times the data to halve the error. The distribution of estimates piles up ever more tightly around $p_0$. That is what "converges to the true value" means.

6.2 Why It Happens: A Three-Step Argument

Step 1: the sample log-likelihood converges to a fixed curve. The average log-likelihood is

$$\frac{1}{n}\,\ell_n(\theta)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\log f(x_i;\theta).$$

This is an average of iid terms. By the Law of Large Numbers, for each fixed $\theta$ it converges to

$$\ell(\theta)=E_{\theta_0}\big[\log f(X;\theta)\big],$$

where the expectation is taken under the true distribution, with parameter $\theta_0$.

Step 2: that limiting curve peaks at the true parameter. Compare it with its value at $\theta_0$:

$$\ell(\theta)-\ell(\theta_0)=E_{\theta_0}\!\left[\log\frac{f(X;\theta)}{f(X;\theta_0)}\right]=-\,\mathrm{KL}\big(f_{\theta_0}\,\|\,f_{\theta}\big)\;\le\;0 .$$

The KL divergence is never negative, and it is zero only when $f_\theta = f_{\theta_0}$. So if different parameter values give different distributions (identifiability), $\theta_0$ is the unique maximiser of $\ell(\theta)$.

Step 3: the peak of the sample curve follows the peak of the limit curve. The curve $\frac{1}{n}\ell_n(\theta)$ is a noisy version of $\ell(\theta)$, and the noise shrinks as $n$ grows. Under regularity conditions (for example, uniform convergence over a compact parameter set), the maximiser $\hat{\theta}_n$ is pulled to the maximiser of $\ell(\theta)$, which is $\theta_0$. This is consistency:

$$\hat{\theta}_n \xrightarrow{\;p\;} \theta_0 .$$

6.3 How Fast: Asymptotic Normality and the Cramér–Rao Bound

At the maximum the score is zero, $\ell_n'(\hat{\theta})=0$. A first-order Taylor expansion of the score around $\theta_0$ gives

$$\hat{\theta}-\theta_0\;\approx\;-\frac{\ell_n'(\theta_0)}{\ell_n''(\theta_0)} .$$
  • Numerator: $\ell_n'(\theta_0)$ is a sum of $n$ iid score terms with mean zero and variance $n\,I(\theta_0)$, where $I(\theta_0)$ is the Fisher information. By the Central Limit Theorem it is approximately normal.
  • Denominator: $\ell_n''(\theta_0)\approx -n\,I(\theta_0)$, by the Law of Large Numbers again.

Putting the two together:

$$\sqrt{n}\,\big(\hat{\theta}-\theta_0\big)\;\xrightarrow{\;d\;}\;N\!\left(0,\;\frac{1}{I(\theta_0)}\right).$$

This is the asymptotic normality listed in Section 5. The variance $1/I(\theta_0)$ is the Cramér–Rao lower bound, which is why MLE is called asymptotically efficient.

Check with the poll: For a Bernoulli parameter, $I(p)=\dfrac{1}{p(1-p)}$, so the limiting variance of $\hat{p}$ is $p(1-p)/n$. This reproduces the standard errors in the table above.

Check with a Gaussian mean: $\hat{\mu}=\bar{x}$ has variance $\sigma^2/n$, again equal to $1/(n\,I(\mu))$ with $I(\mu)=1/\sigma^2$.

6.4 Caveats

  • Convergence is in probability. For any $\varepsilon>0$, $P\big(|\hat{\theta}_n-\theta_0|>\varepsilon\big)\to 0$. A particular sample can still land far from $\theta_0$; it just becomes increasingly unlikely.
  • Identifiability is required. If two different parameter values give the same distribution, Step 2 has no unique peak, and there is no single true value to converge to.
  • Regularity conditions are required. Consistency can fail when the number of parameters grows with $n$. A classic case is the Neyman–Scott problem: with one unknown mean per pair of observations and a shared variance $\sigma^2$, the MLE of $\sigma^2$ converges to $\sigma^2/2$, not $\sigma^2$.
  • Efficiency is an asymptotic statement. In finite samples the MLE can be biased. The Gaussian variance MLE, which divides by $n$, has expectation $\frac{n-1}{n}\sigma^2$. It is the large-$n$ behaviour that is unbiased and attains the Cramér–Rao bound.
