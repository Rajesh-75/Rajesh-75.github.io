Adi Shankara’s doctrine of Brahman and Maya forms the core of Advaita Vedanta, a non-dualistic Hindu philosophy. It states that the unchanging, infinite consciousness (Brahman) is the only absolute reality, while the diverse, ever-changing universe we perceive is a projection of illusion (Maya). [1, 2, 3, 4]  
1. Brahman: The Ultimate Reality 
In Shankara’s philosophy, Brahman is the sole, singular, and unchanging reality. It is entirely free from all attributes, distinctions, or limitations, making it Nirguna Brahman (the featureless Absolute). 

• True Nature: Described as Sat-Chit-Ananda (pure existence, pure consciousness, and pure bliss). 
• Atman: The individual soul (Atman) is not a part of Brahman, but is completely identical to it. The apparent separation we experience is caused by ignorance. [3, 4, 6, 7, 8]  

2. Maya: The Veil of Illusion 
Maya is Shankara's explanation for how the one, indivisible Brahman appears as a manifold, changing universe. It is both a concealing and projecting power (Shakti). 

• Not Pure Nothingness: Maya is not absolutely unreal; it exists on an empirical level. It functions practically in time and space, but it lacks ultimate, eternal reality. 
• The Rope and the Snake: Shankara famously used the metaphor of a coiled rope in the dark. Because of the darkness, one might mistake the rope for a snake and feel fear. The "snake" is real to the observer's experience in that moment, but it vanishes once the light is turned on and the true nature of the "rope" is realized. 
• Jagat (The World): Because of Maya, the material world appears distinct and separate, but it is ultimately a superimposed illusion (mithya) upon the substratum of Brahman. [2, 9]  

3. Avidya (Ignorance) and Ishvara (God) 
Maya operates at both the cosmic and individual levels, leading to two distinct phenomena: 

• Avidya (Individual Level): Maya clouds individual human perception, causing people to identify with their transient bodies, minds, and egos rather than their true, infinite self (Atman). 
• Ishvara (Cosmic Level): When the supreme, attribute-less Brahman is viewed through the lens of Maya, it appears as Sagun Brahman or Ishvara (the personal Creator, God). To a person still bound by Maya, Ishvara is the ruler of the universe. [3, 10, 11, 12, 13]  

4. The Path to Liberation (Moksha) 
According to Advaita, liberation is not about going somewhere else or achieving something new, but simply removing the veil of Maya to realize what has always been true. 

• By practicing discrimination (Viveka) and acquiring spiritual knowledge (Jnana), the individual realizes the ultimate truth: "Aham Brahmasmi" (I am Brahman). Once this realization occurs, the illusion of duality dissolves, and the soul rests in its natural, liberated state. [3, 7, 9, 14, 15]

In Advaita Vedanta, practicing discrimination is called Viveka. It is the intellectual and spiritual practice of separating what is real from what is unreal. [1] 
Here is how you can practice Viveka in daily life based on Adi Shankara's teachings. [2] 
## 1. The Core Principle: Sat vs. Asat
The foundation of Viveka is constantly distinguishing between two categories:

* Sat (The Real): That which never changes, has no beginning, and no end. This is pure consciousness (Brahman/Atman).
* Asat (The Unreal): That which changes, is temporary, and depends on something else to exist. This includes the physical world, your body, and your thoughts.

## 2. Daily Practices for Viveka
You can cultivate this mindset through specific mental shifts throughout the day:

* De-link your identity from the body: When you feel hungry, sick, or tired, remind yourself: "My body is experiencing hunger, but I am the witness of this body." You are the changeless observer, not the changing physical frame.
* Observe your emotions neutrally: When anger, anxiety, or joy arises, do not say "I am angry." Instead, say: "An ocean wave of anger is passing through my mind. I am the steady ocean beneath it." This breaks your identification with temporary mental states.
* Question worldly permanence: When you enjoy material comforts, wealth, or relationships, acknowledge their value but remind yourself of their nature: "This object brings comfort now, but it is bound by time and will eventually change or disappear." This reduces dependency and anxiety.

## 3. The Neti-Neti Method
This is the classic Vedantic meditation technique of negation. It means "Not this, not this."

   1. Sit quietly and look inward.
   2. Look at your body and realize: "I can perceive this body, so I am not this body."
   3. Look at your breath and realize: "I can observe my breath, so I am not this breath."
   4. Look at your thoughts and memories: "I am the one aware of these thoughts, so I cannot be the thoughts themselves."
   5. What remains after negating everything changeful is the pure, witnessing consciousness—the Atman.

## 4. Prerequisites for Effective Discrimination
Shankara emphasized that Viveka requires a calm, prepared mind. To make your discrimination sharp, cultivate the fourfold qualifications (Sadhana Chatushtaya):

* Vairagya (Dispassion): Letting go of the desire for temporary, worldly rewards.
* Shatsampat (Six Virtues): Calming the mind, controlling the senses, practicing endurance, developing faith, and staying focused.
* Mumukshutva (Longing): Having an intense, sincere desire for spiritual freedom and truth.

In Advaita Vedanta, negative emotions like fear, anger, lust, frustration, and greed are called Kleshas (mental afflictions) or Shadripus (the six internal enemies). [1, 2, 3, 4] 
According to Adi Shankara, these emotions do not belong to your true self (Atman). They arise purely because Maya and Avidya (ignorance) trick you into believing you are a limited body and mind.
Here is the exact chain reaction of why these emotions occur in us:
## 1. The Root Cause: Avidya (Spiritual Ignorance)
The process begins when you forget your true nature. You are actually the infinite, changeless Atman. However, ignorance makes you identify strictly with your limited ego (Ahamkara), physical body, and mind. Once you believe "I am this fragile, needy body," you immediately feel incomplete and vulnerable.
## 2. The Birth of Lust (Kama) and Greed (Lobha)
Because the ego feels incomplete, it constantly searches outside itself for happiness, security, and fulfillment.

* Lust/Desire (Kama): The mind projects the idea that a specific object, person, or experience will make you happy. You develop an intense attachment and craving to possess it.
* Greed (Lobha): Once you acquire the object of desire, the ego fears losing it or wants more of it to maintain that temporary high. [5, 6, 7, 8, 9] 

## 3. The Shift to Anger (Krodha) and Frustration
This step is famously explained in the Bhagavad Gita (Chapter 2, Verses 62-63) as the "Ladder of Fall." [10, 11] 

* Anger & Frustration: When your desires or greed face an obstacle—such as a person blocking your way, or circumstances not going your way—your desire gets blocked. Blocked desire instantly transforms into anger and frustration. [12, 13, 14, 15, 16] 

## 4. The Manifestation of Fear (Bhaya)
Shankara emphasizes a profound truth in the Upanishads: "Wherever there is duality, there is fear."

* Fear (Bhaya): When you live in Maya, you see the world as separate from you ("me" versus "them"). Once you perceive others as separate, you automatically fear them. You fear that people, time, disease, or death will steal your possessions, your loved ones, or your physical life. [17] 

## Summary of the Chain Reaction
$$\text{Ignorance (Avidya)} \rightarrow \text{Ego Identity} \rightarrow \text{Desire/Greed} \rightarrow \text{Obstruction} \rightarrow \text{Anger/Frustration/Fear}$$ 
## The Vedantic Solution
These emotions are like waves on the surface of the ocean. They belong to the mind, not to you. Through Viveka (discrimination), you look at anger or fear and say, "I am the witness observing this anger; therefore, I am not the anger." By refusing to feed the emotion with your identity, it naturally loses its power and dissolves. [18]

In his definitive commentaries on the Bhagavad Gita (specifically Chapter 2) and the Upanishads, Shankara explains that grief arises from misidentifying a temporary physical form as the eternal reality. [1, 2, 3] 
To overcome this deep emotional pain, Shankara advises shifting your perspective using the sharp lens of Advaita Vedanta through the following principles:
## 1. Deconstruct the Target of Your Grief
Shankara asks a radical question in his commentary on Gita Verse 2.11: Who exactly are you crying for? You have only two choices, and logically, neither one deserves grief: [2, 4] 

* The Body: The physical body is a temporary combination of elements bound to disintegrate by its very nature. Mourning its decay is like crying because a sunset occurs or because a ceramic pot eventually breaks. It is unavoidable. [3, 5, 6, 7, 8] 
* The Atman (Soul): The true nature of your loved one is pure consciousness (Atman), which is unborn, undying, and indestructible. Weapons cannot cut it, and death cannot touch it. Therefore, the real person has not ceased to exist; only their physical vessel has dropped away. [6, 9, 10] 

Mourning either the naturally perishable body or the completely imperishable soul is an error of judgment. [2, 4] 
## 2. Recognize Duality as the Root of Fear
Shankara frequently cites the Upanishadic maxim: "Wherever there is a sense of 'otherness' or duality, fear arises." [11] 

* When you view your loved one as an independent, separate entity who belonged to "you," you live in constant anxiety of losing them.
* Once death occurs, the ego perceives a massive, painful void.
* The Solution: Recognize that both you and your loved one are expressions of the exact same cosmic consciousness (Brahman). A wave does not truly disappear when it crashes back into the ocean; it simply returns to its source. You are eternally connected to them in the unified field of consciousness, making separation an illusion. [10, 11, 12] 

## 3. Expose the "Maniac" Inconsistency of the Mind
In a famous section of his commentary, Shankara compares a grieving person to a maniac who speaks words of wisdom but acts with complete foolishness. [2] 

* You might intellectually know and say that "everyone must die," yet when it happens to a relative, you act shocked and completely paralyzed by sorrow. [2] 
* Shankara demands consistency: apply the spiritual truth you know to your actual life. Real wisdom (Panditya) means remaining steady because you know the true nature of life and death. [2, 4, 13] 

## 4. Understand that Grief Feeds Samsara
Shankara emphasizes that deep, prolonged grief and delusion are the heavy "seeds" that bind you to the painful cycle of birth and death (Samsara). [14] 

* When you grieve excessively, you reinforce the illusion that the material world is the absolute truth.
* Your intense attachment creates emotional blocks that drag your own consciousness down.
* By practicing Viveka (discrimination) and recognizing death as a mere changing of clothes for the soul, you cut the chord of grief and free both yourself and the departed soul from the heavy energetic ties of worldly attachment. [7, 9, 14, 15, 16] 

## Practical Advice from the Tradition
To process the loss of a dear person according to this philosophy:

   1. Acknowledge the biological emotion as a temporary wave passing over your mind, but do not internalize it as your ultimate reality. [17] 
   2. Meditate on the timeless nature of the consciousness that animated your loved one, rather than brooding over their missing physical frame. [9, 13] 
   3. Honor their journey by channeling your emotional energy into selfless service and spiritual study, utilizing the experience of loss to deepen your own search for the eternal. [7, 13]
