Measures of Central Tendency Series

Average (Arithmetic Mean)

An average (Mean) is a simple figure which sums up the characteristics of a whole group of figures. An average is more or less a central value around which various values cluster.

Mathematical Definition

The Arithmetic Mean is the sum of all observations divided by the total number of observations.

$$ \text{Mean } (\bar{X}) = \frac{x_1 + x_2 + \dots + x_n}{n} = \frac{\sum x}{n} $$

Computation by Data Type

Series 1

1. Individual Observations (Discrete Data)

Used when individual values are given. Simply add all values and divide by the count ($n$).

Example: Average income of 10 individuals (10, 90, 85, 103, 11, 29, 84, 15, 35, 80).

$$ \bar{X} = \frac{\sum x}{N} = \frac{542}{10} = 54.2 \text{ Rs} $$
Series 2

2. Discrete Frequency Distribution

Used when values ($x$) have associated frequencies ($f$). We calculate the product of each value and its frequency ($fx$) to find the total.

Properties & Variations

Properties of the Arithmetic Mean & Other Averages

Key Properties of the Mean:

  • Sum of Deviations: The algebraic sum of deviations of all observations from their arithmetic mean is always zero: $\sum (x - \bar{X}) = 0$.
  • Change of Origin and Scale: If a constant $c$ is added/subtracted from each observation, the mean changes by $c$. If each observation is multiplied/divided by $c$, the mean is similarly multiplied/divided.
  • Combined Mean: If two groups have means $\bar{X}_1, \bar{X}_2$ and sizes $N_1, N_2$, the combined mean is: $$ \bar{X}_{12} = \frac{N_1\bar{X}_1 + N_2\bar{X}_2}{N_1 + N_2} $$
  • Sensitivity to Outliers: The mean is pulled strongly by outliers because every value is factored directly into the sum. For skewed data with extreme values, the mean becomes unrepresentative.

Alternative Means:

  • Weighted Mean: Used when different observations carry varying levels of importance ($w_i$): $$ \bar{X}_w = \frac{\sum w_i x_i}{\sum w_i} $$
  • Geometric Mean (GM): Useful for growth rates and compounding: $$ GM = \sqrt[n]{x_1 x_2 \dots x_n} $$
  • Harmonic Mean (HM): Useful for rates and ratios. $$ HM = \frac{N}{\sum \left(\frac{1}{x}\right)} $$
    💡 ML Connection: The Harmonic Mean is precisely the formula used to compute the F1 Score in machine learning (the harmonic mean of Precision and Recall).

Median

Median is defined as the size of the middle item when items are arranged in ascending order of magnitude. Unlike the mean, the median is not pulled by outliers, making it robust for skewed data.

Case 1

1. Individual Observations

For ungrouped data, arrange the values in ascending order.

$$ \text{Position} = \left( \frac{N+1}{2} \right)^{th} \text{ item} $$

Note on Even N: If $N$ is even, $\frac{N+1}{2}$ results in a half-integer (e.g., $4.5^{\text{th}}$). In this case, the median is calculated by taking the arithmetic mean of the two middle values surrounding that position.

Worked Example (Even N): For data [12, 15, 18, 22, 25, 30] ($N=6$): Position = $\frac{6+1}{2} = 3.5^{\text{th}}$. Average the 3rd (18) and 4th (22) values $\rightarrow$ Median = $\frac{18 + 22}{2} = 20$.

Case 2

2. Discrete Frequency Distribution

In the discrete case, we use cumulative frequency to locate the item position.

$$ \text{Position} = \left( \frac{\sum f + 1}{2} \right)^{th} \text{ item} $$
Case 3

3. Continuous Series

For grouped data, we first find the median class using $N/2$. Then, we apply the interpolation formula:

$$ \text{Median} = L_1 + \frac{\frac{N}{2} - cf}{f} \times c $$

Where:

  • $L_1$: Lower limit of the median class.
  • $N$: Total frequency ($\sum f$).
  • $cf$: Cumulative frequency of the class just preceding the median class.
  • $f$: Frequency of the median class.
  • $c$: Class interval width.
Quantiles

Quartiles, Percentiles, and Interquartile Range (IQR)

Just as the median splits data into two halves, other quantiles split data into finer segments:

  • Quartiles ($Q_1, Q_2, Q_3$): Divide data into 4 equal parts. $Q_1$ is the 25th percentile, $Q_2$ is the Median (50th percentile), and $Q_3$ is the 75th percentile.
  • Percentiles ($P_1, P_2, \dots, P_{99}$): Divide data into 100 equal parts.
  • Interquartile Range (IQR): The spread of the middle 50% of the data, defined as: $$ \text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 $$
  • Quartile Deviation (Semi-Interquartile Range): $$ QD = \frac{Q_3 - Q_1}{2} $$

Note: These metrics form the backbone of box plots and robust outlier boundaries.

Mode

Mode is the value of that item that occurs the greatest number of times in a distribution.

Example: In the series $23, 35, 42, 62, 53, 35, 28, 42, 35, 23, 42, 35$, the value $35$ appears most frequently. Therefore, the Mode is 35.

Empirical Relationship

Asymmetrical Distributions

When no item appears more times than any other, the mode is "ill-defined." In such asymmetrical distributions, the mode is calculated using the empirical formula:

$$ \text{Mode} = 3 \times \text{Median} - 2 \times \text{Mean} $$
Continuous Case

Continuous Distributions (Finding the Modal Class)

For grouped data, we first identify the modal class (the class interval carrying the highest frequency). Then, the mode is calculated as:

$$ \text{Mode} = L_1 + \frac{(f_1 - f_0) \times c}{(2f_1 - f_0 - f_2)} $$

Where:

  • $L_1$: Lower limit of the modal class.
  • $f_1$: Frequency of the modal class.
  • $f_0$: Frequency of the class just preceding the modal class.
  • $f_2$: Frequency of the class just succeeding the modal class.
  • $c$: Class interval width.

Measures of Dispersion

Measures of central tendency give the center of a dataset, but they do not reveal how scattered or spread out the values are. Measures of dispersion quantify this variability.

Dispersion Metrics

Other Key Measures of Dispersion

  • Range: The difference between the highest and lowest values in the dataset. ($Range = X_{\max} - X_{\min}$). Highly sensitive to outliers.
  • Quartile Deviation (IQR / 2): Measures the dispersion of the middle 50% of data. Extremely robust against extreme outliers.
  • Mean Deviation (MD): The average of the absolute deviations from a central value (Mean or Median): $$ MD_{\text{Mean}} = \frac{\sum |x - \bar{X}|}{N} $$

Standard Deviation & Variance

Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) is defined as the square root of the mean of the squares of deviations taken from the Arithmetic Mean. It is the most widely used measure of dispersion.

The relationship between Variance and Standard Deviation is simple:

$$ \text{Variance} = \sigma^2 $$ $$ \text{Standard Deviation} = \sqrt{\text{Variance}} $$
Crucial Distinction

Population vs. Sample Variance

Formulas differ depending on whether you analyze a complete population or a sample drawn from it:

  • Population Variance ($\sigma^2$): Divides by $N$ (total items in population). $$ \sigma^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{X})^2}{N} $$
  • Sample Variance ($s^2$): Divides by $n - 1$ (degrees of freedom correction to eliminate bias in sample estimation): $$ s^2 = \frac{\sum (x - \bar{X})^2}{n - 1} $$

💡 Note: The sample variance notation ($s^2$) is precisely the version utilized across your Hypothesis Testing pages.

Individual Observations

Mathematical Formula (Population)

$$ \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum (x - \bar{X})^2}{N}} $$

Worked Numerical Example: Calculate SD for dataset [2, 4, 4, 4, 5, 5, 7, 9] ($N=8$).

  • Mean ($\bar{X}$) = $\frac{2+4+4+4+5+5+7+9}{8} = \frac{40}{8} = 5$.
  • Squared deviations $(x - \bar{X})^2$: $(2-5)^2=9, (4-5)^2=1, \dots, (9-5)^2=16$. Sum = $9 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 0 + 0 + 4 + 16 = 32$.
  • Variance ($\sigma^2$) = $\frac{32}{8} = 4$. Standard Deviation ($\sigma$) = $\sqrt{4} = 2$.
Short-cut Method

Calculation using Assumed Mean (A)

$$ \sigma = \sqrt{\frac{\sum fd^2}{N} - \left( \frac{\sum fd}{N} \right)^2} $$
Interpretation

Why the Mean needs the Standard Deviation

The Mean gives us the location of the data, but the Standard Deviation (SD) gives us the context. One without the other can be misleading.

  • Low SD: The data points are close to the Mean. The average is a reliable representative of the group.
  • High SD: The data points are spread out far from the Mean. This suggests that the average may not be a good representation because of high volatility or outliers.

The "Consistency" Rule:

Scenario Mean Salary SD Conclusion
Company A Rs 500,000 Rs 2,000 Consistent: Most people earn very close to Rs 500,000.
Company B Rs 500,000 Rs 250,000 Inconsistent: There are likely very high and very low earners.
Comparison Tool

Coefficient of Variation (CV)

To mathematically measure consistency across different scales, use the Coefficient of Variation:

$$ CV = \left( \frac{\sigma}{\bar{X}} \right) \times 100 $$

Interpretation: Lower CV means higher stability and uniformity; higher CV indicates high relative volatility.

ML Applications

Machine Learning Connections & Preprocessing

  • Standardization (Z-Scores): Centers data to mean 0 and scales variance to 1 using mean and standard deviation: $$ z = \frac{x - \bar{X}}{\sigma} $$
  • Robust Scaling: Uses the Median and Interquartile Range (IQR) instead of Mean and SD. Highly effective when datasets contain extreme outliers.
  • Imputing Missing Values: Missing numerical features are often filled using the mean (for normal distributions) or the median (for skewed distributions or data with outliers).
  • Outlier Capping / Detection: Data points falling beyond $Q_3 + 1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ or below $Q_1 - 1.5 \times \text{IQR}$ are classified as outliers.

Measures of Skewness and Kurtosis

While Mean and SD tell us about the center and spread, Skewness and Kurtosis describe the Shape of the probability distribution relative to a normal distribution ("bell curve").

Shape: Symmetry

1. Measures of Skewness

Skewness refers to the lack of symmetry in a distribution.

  • Symmetrical: $\text{Mean} = \text{Median} = \text{Mode}$.
  • Positive Skew (Right-skewed): Tail stretches right. $\text{Mean} > \text{Median} > \text{Mode}$.
  • Negative Skew (Left-skewed): Tail stretches left. $\text{Mean} < \text{Median} < \text{Mode}$.

Karl Pearson's Coefficient of Skewness ($S_k$):

$$ S_k = \frac{\text{Mean} - \text{Mode}}{\sigma} \quad \text{or} \quad S_k = \frac{3(\text{Mean} - \text{Median})}{\sigma} $$

Worked Example: If $\text{Mean} = 50$, $\text{Mode} = 44$, and $\sigma = 3$, then $S_k = \frac{50 - 44}{3} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$ (indicates strong positive skew).

Bowley’s Quartile Skewness Coefficient:

Based on quartiles, useful when extreme values distort the mean:

$$ S_q = \frac{Q_3 + Q_1 - 2Q_2}{Q_3 - Q_1} $$
Shape: Peakedness & Moments

2. Measures of Kurtosis & Moments

Moments are statistical descriptors of the shape of a set of points. The $r^{\text{th}}$ central moment ($\mu_r$) about the mean is:

$$ \mu_r = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (x_i - \bar{X})^r $$

Kurtosis measures the "tailedness" relative to a normal distribution using standardized moments:

Type Description Condition ($\beta_2$) Excess Kurtosis ($\gamma_2 = \beta_2 - 3$)
Leptokurtic Sharply peaked with heavy tails (outlier-prone). $\beta_2 > 3$ $\gamma_2 > 0$
Mesokurtic Normal Distribution benchmark. $\beta_2 = 3$ $\gamma_2 = 0$
Platykurtic Flat-topped with thin tails. $\beta_2 < 3$ $\gamma_2 < 0$

Formula for Kurtosis ($\beta_2$):

$$ \beta_2 = \frac{\mu_4}{\mu_2^2} $$
