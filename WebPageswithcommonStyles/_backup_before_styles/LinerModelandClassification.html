Linear Models

The Linear Regression Model

Regression, broadly, is the task of predicting a continuous numerical quantity from one or more known inputs — predicting a house's price from its size, a student's exam score from hours studied, or here, a year's rainfall from the year itself. Linear Regression is the specific assumption that this relationship can be captured by a straight line (or, with more inputs, a flat plane/hyperplane): the target is assumed to change by a constant amount for every unit change in the input, rather than curving, plateauing, or behaving erratically.

This is best understood as a functional mapping from input to output: once the line's two numbers are fixed, feeding in any $x$ deterministically produces a corresponding $y$ — the model has no other behavior. With a single input variable, that mapping is $y=wx+c$; with several inputs $x_1,\dots,x_p$ (e.g., predicting rainfall from year and average temperature), the same idea extends to $y = w_1x_1+w_2x_2+\cdots+w_px_p+c$ — a weighted sum of the inputs plus one intercept. This page works with a single input to keep the algebra visible, but everything derived below generalizes directly to that multi-input case.

Neither the line's slope nor its intercept is known in advance — determining them from data is the entire learning task. That's what the rest of this page does: starting from the concrete rainfall-vs-year case below, working out exactly how those two unknown numbers get estimated, and what assumptions that estimation secretly relies on.

The model $y = wx + c$ expresses rainfall ($y$, the dependent variable — the quantity we want to predict) as a function of year ($x$, the independent variable — the quantity we already know). Here $w$ is the slope, and $c$ is the intercept — called the bias in ML/DL terminology.

A terminology collision worth knowing: "bias" means two different things depending on context. In ML/DL, bias is the intercept term itself — exactly $c$ here. In classical statistics, the bias of an estimator is something else entirely: $E[\hat\theta]-\theta$, how far an estimation procedure's expected result is from the true parameter on average. Both usages are legitimate; they just aren't the same concept. Also worth being precise about: whether $c=0$ or not is an empirical fact about the specific data being fit, not a general rule — some real relationships genuinely do pass through the origin, others don't.
The Three Components, for Linear Regression

This page follows the same three-part pattern used for every algorithm in this series (see the ML Pipeline page's Model Training section):

  1. Model — $y = wx + c$, defined above: the hypothesis that rainfall relates to year through a straight line.
  2. Cost Function (Loss) — the squared-error criterion $S(w,c)=\sum_n(t_n-wx_n-c)^2$, introduced next, used to judge how good any candidate $(w,c)$ is.
  3. Optimization — here, solved in closed form via the normal equations: differentiate $S(w,c)$, set both partial derivatives to zero, solve directly. No iteration or learning rate is needed — worth noting precisely because it's the exception rather than the rule; most models covered later in this series (starting with Logistic Regression) have no closed-form solution and require iterative optimization instead.

How Good Is a Given $(w, c)$? Measuring Closeness

To choose the values of $w$ and $c$ that give the best-fitting line, we need some way to compare candidates. The best line is the one that passes as close as possible to all $N$ data points $(x_n, t_n)$, $n=1,\dots,N$ — not just one of them. Here $t_n$ denotes the observed target, the true rainfall for year $x_n$ (you'll see $y_n$ used for the same quantity elsewhere on this page; the two are used interchangeably).

A common way to measure how close a particular candidate line gets to a single point is the squared difference between the observed value $t_n$ and the value the model predicts, $f(x_n,w,c) = wx_n + c$:

$$\big(t_n - f(x_n, w, c)\big)^2$$

The smaller this number, the closer the model's prediction is to what was actually observed at that point. Squaring does two things here: it makes the quantity non-negative (so an overshoot and an undershoot of equal size both count as errors, rather than cancelling out the way plain differences would), and it penalizes larger errors more than proportionally — a residual twice as large contributes four times the cost, not twice.

Judging $(w,c)$ against the whole dataset, rather than one point, just means summing this per-point squared error over all $N$ points. That total is exactly the $S(w,c)$ below, and minimizing it is what Least Squares does.

Both $w$ and $c$ are unknown and must be estimated from the data — not just $w$. The classical method for doing this is Least Squares: choose $(w,c)$ to minimize the sum of squared residuals,

$$S(w,c) = \sum_{i=1}^{N}\big(y_i - wx_i - c\big)^2$$

Setting both partial derivatives to zero:

$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial c} = -2\sum_i (y_i - wx_i - c) = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; c = \bar{y} - w\bar{x}$$ $$\frac{\partial S}{\partial w} = -2\sum_i x_i(y_i - wx_i - c) = 0$$

Substituting the first result into the second and solving gives the closed-form estimate for the slope:

$$w = \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})(y_i-\bar{y})}{\sum_i (x_i-\bar{x})^2}$$

This is exactly the calculation behind the fitted line in the rainfall figure below — $w\approx3.28$ cm/year, $c\approx81.95$ cm.

Least Squares is the classical way to fit this model, but not the only one: MLE under Gaussian noise (covered below) gives the identical $(w,c)$, and Regularization (covered next on this page) deliberately optimizes a different objective by adding a penalty term. Linear Regression is fundamentally the modeling choice — a straight-line functional mapping from $x$ to $y$ — with Least Squares being the most common, but not the sole, way to fit its parameters.

Ordinary least squares treats fitting a line as a purely geometric/algebraic problem: minimize $\sum_i (y_i - wx_i)^2$, take the derivative, solve. Nothing random involved — it's optimization, not statistics.

The probabilistic reframing changes the starting assumption: instead of just having data points, you assume there's a true underlying relationship, and each observed target is that true value plus random noise:

$$t_i = wx_i + \epsilon_i, \qquad \epsilon_i \sim N(0, \sigma^2)$$

This is a genuinely different object — a generative model. It says: "if you gave me the true $w$ and $\sigma^2$, here's the full probability distribution I'd expect to see for $t_i$" — specifically $t_i \sim N(wx_i, \sigma^2)$. Once you have that, you can write a likelihood — the probability of the entire observed dataset, as a function of the unknown $w$ — and ask which $w$ makes the data you actually saw most probable. That's MLE.

Step 1 — Construct the Equation From the Data

Using $S(w,c) = \sum_{n=1}^{10}(t_n - wu_n - c)^2$ from above, plug in the sums needed for the normal equations. From the table: $N=10$, $\sum u_n = 45$, $\sum t_n = 967$, $\sum u_n^2 = 285$, $\sum u_n t_n = 4622$.

Step 2 — Differentiate and Equate to Zero

Setting $\partial S/\partial c=0$ and $\partial S/\partial w=0$ (as derived earlier) gives the two normal equations:

$$\sum t_n = w\sum u_n + Nc \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 967 = 45w + 10c \qquad \text{(i)}$$ $$\sum u_n t_n = w\sum u_n^2 + c\sum u_n \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; 4622 = 285w + 45c \qquad \text{(ii)}$$

Step 3 — Determine $w$ and Construct the Model

From (i): $c = 96.7 - 4.5w$. Substituting into (ii):

$$4622 = 285w + 45(96.7 - 4.5w) = 82.5w + 4351.5$$ $$w = \frac{270.5}{82.5} \approx 3.28$$

Back-substituting: $c = 96.7 - 4.5(3.28) \approx 81.95$. So the fitted model, in terms of $u=\text{year}-2016$, is:

$$\hat{t} = 3.28u + 81.95, \qquad \text{i.e.} \qquad \hat{t} = 3.28(\text{year}-2016) + 81.95$$

This is exactly the blue line drawn through the scatter in the figure above.

Step 4 — Find a Year for a Given Rainfall Value

The fitted equation runs both ways: given a year, it predicts rainfall; given a target rainfall, it can be solved for the year that would be expected to reach it. Suppose we want to know when rainfall might first reach 120 cm. Set $\hat{t}=120$ and solve for $u$:

$$120 = 3.28u + 81.95 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; u = \frac{38.05}{3.28} \approx 11.6 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; \text{year} \approx 2016+11.6 \approx 2028$$

Caution: the fitted data only covers 2016–2025 ($u=0$ to $9$). $u\approx11.6$ falls outside that range, so this is an extrapolation, not an interpolation — the line is assumed to keep behaving the same way beyond the data it was fit on, which is a much weaker assumption than predicting a year that falls inside the observed range.

Step 5 — A Better Test: Interpolating a Withheld Year

Extrapolating to 2028 has no ground truth to check against — we simply don't know what actually happens there. A more convincing demonstration holds out a year we do have data for, fits the model as if it were missing, predicts it, and then compares the prediction to the real value. This is interpolation, since the withheld year sits inside the range the rest of the data still covers.

Remove 2021 ($u=5$, $t=102$) from the fitting data, leaving 9 points. Recomputing the sums: $\sum u_n=40$, $\sum t_n=865$, $\sum u_n^2=260$, $\sum u_nt_n=4112$, $N=9$. Solving the normal equations exactly as before:

$$w = \frac{9(4112)-40(865)}{9(260)-40^2} = \frac{2408}{740} \approx 3.254, \qquad c = \frac{865-3.254(40)}{9} \approx 81.65$$

Predicting the withheld year, $u=5$:

$$\hat{t} = 3.254(5) + 81.65 \approx 97.92\text{ cm}$$

The actual observed 2021 rainfall was 102 cm — a model that never saw 2021 at all landed within about 4 cm of the truth. That's a genuinely checkable result, and it works precisely because 2021 sits well inside the range of years the remaining 9 points still cover — the opposite situation from predicting 2028.

A passing note: the fitted line above is a degree-1 polynomial in $x$. Nothing stops us from fitting a higher-degree polynomial instead — degree 9 through these 10 points would pass through every single one, driving the training error to zero. That's not actually better: a curve that contorts itself to hit every noisy point exactly stops representing the underlying trend and starts memorizing the noise, performing badly on any year it wasn't fit to. This is the same underfitting/overfitting trade-off from the ML Pipeline page's Model Training section — degree here plays the same role "model complexity" does there.

What Is Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)?

Maximum Likelihood Estimation is a method for estimating a model's unknown parameters by asking a different question than Least Squares does: instead of directly minimizing a distance criterion, assume a probability distribution for how the data was generated, then pick the parameter values that make the data actually observed the most probable outcome under that distribution.

A Tiny Concrete Example

Say you have three points: $(x_1,y_1)=(1,3)$, $(x_2,y_2)=(2,5)$, $(x_3,y_3)=(3,6)$, and a model with no intercept: $y_i = wx_i + \epsilon_i$, where $\epsilon_i \sim N(0,\sigma^2)$.

Figure: The candidate line $y=2x$ against the three observed points. The dashed segments are the residuals $\epsilon_i = y_i - wx_i$ that a Gaussian noise assumption converts into probability densities.

Step 1 — What Does "Probability of the Data" Even Mean Here?

Pick a candidate value, say $w=2$. Under that guess, the predicted values are $2, 4, 6$. The actual observed values were $3, 5, 6$. So the noise that "must have happened," under this candidate $w$, is: $\epsilon_1=1$, $\epsilon_2=1$, $\epsilon_3=0$.

Now — we assumed $\epsilon \sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. That assumption tells us exactly how probable each of those specific noise values is. The Gaussian density function gives us a number for "how likely is a noise value of exactly 1" versus "how likely is a noise value of exactly 0." That's the whole trick: the noise assumption converts a residual into a probability (density) value.

Step 2 — Multiply Them Together

For a given point, the density of observing $y_i$ given $x_i$ and $w$ is:

$$N(y_i; wx_i, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp\left(-\frac{(y_i-wx_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$

Assuming the three data points are independent, the probability of seeing all three together is the product of their individual densities:

$$L(w) = \prod_{i=1}^{3} N(y_i; wx_i,\sigma^2)$$

This product, as a function of $w$, is the likelihood. Different candidate $w$'s give different residuals, which give different density values, which give a different overall product. MLE says: find the $w$ that makes this product as large as possible.

Step 3 — Where the Squared Error Actually Comes From

Taking the log of that product (logs turn products into sums, and don't change where the maximum sits):

$$\log L(w) = \sum_{i=1}^{3} \left[-\frac{1}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{(y_i-wx_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right]$$

The first term inside the brackets doesn't depend on $w$ at all — it's just a constant that shifts every candidate $w$'s score equally, so it can't affect which $w$ wins. Strip it out, and maximizing what's left is:

$$\max_w \left(-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i-wx_i)^2\right) \;\equiv\; \min_w \sum_i (y_i-wx_i)^2$$

That last step — maximizing a negative thing is the same as minimizing the positive thing inside it — is the entire punchline. The sum-of-squared-residuals term didn't get inserted by hand; it fell straight out of the algebra, purely because we chose a Gaussian for the noise. If you'd instead assumed the noise followed a Laplace distribution (density $\propto e^{-|\epsilon|/b}$), the exact same three steps would have landed you minimizing $\sum_i |y_i - wx_i|$ instead — absolute error, not squared error. The loss function isn't a free design choice; it's a consequence of what you assumed about the noise.

The One Thing MLE Gets That Pure Least Squares Never Asks For

Notice $\sigma^2$ was sitting there the whole derivation, but least squares never needed it — it dropped out of the equation entirely once we started minimizing. MLE, if you also take the derivative of the log-likelihood with respect to $\sigma^2$ and set that to zero, hands you a second answer for free:

$$\hat{\sigma}^2 = \frac{1}{N}\sum_i(y_i - \hat{w}x_i)^2$$

— literally the average squared residual, using the best-fit $\hat{w}$ you just found. That's the noise-level estimate least squares has no mechanism to produce, because it never had a probabilistic noise model to begin with.

Numerical Example: MLE on the Rainfall Data

This is the same rainfall dataset used for the Least Squares worked example above — the point is to confirm, with real numbers rather than abstract algebra, that MLE reproduces the identical fit and then hands back the extra $\hat\sigma^2$ that Least Squares never computed.

Step 1 — Same Normal Equations, Same $(w,c)$

Maximizing the log-likelihood $\log L(w,c,\sigma^2)$ over $w$ and $c$ reduces to the identical minimization of $S(w,c)=\sum_n(t_n-wu_n-c)^2$ shown in Step 3 earlier — the algebra never used the specific data, only the fact that the constant term drops out. So plugging in the same sums from the Least Squares example ($\sum u_n=45$, $\sum t_n=967$, $\sum u_n^2=285$, $\sum u_nt_n=4622$, $N=10$) gives back exactly the same normal equations, and therefore exactly the same answer:

$$\hat{w} \approx 3.28, \qquad \hat{c} \approx 81.95$$

Same fitted line as before — no surprise there, that equivalence was proven algebraically. The new content starts in Step 2.

Step 4 — Use $\hat\sigma$ for a Prediction Interval (What LS Alone Can't Give You)

This is where "why bother with MLE" gets a concrete answer. Least Squares gives a single-number prediction for a new year; MLE's kept probability model lets that prediction come with a range. For year 2026 ($u=10$):

$$\hat{t} = 3.28(10) + 81.95 = 114.75 \text{ cm}$$

A Least Squares report stops there: "114.75 cm." MLE's underlying model, $t\sim N(\hat{t}, \hat\sigma^2)$, supports an approximate 95% prediction interval of $\hat{t}\pm1.96\hat\sigma$:

$$114.75 \pm 1.96(5.56) \;\approx\; 114.75 \pm 10.9 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; (103.9,\ 125.7)\text{ cm}$$

"Somewhere around 115 cm, most likely between about 104 and 126 cm" is a materially more useful statement than a bare point estimate — and it exists only because the probability model was kept around instead of being discarded once $(w,c)$ were found.

Step 5 — The Same Interval, But Checkable: Interpolating 2021

The 2026 interval above can't be verified — 2026 hasn't happened yet in this dataset. Using the 9-point fit from the interpolation example earlier ($w\approx3.254$, $c\approx81.65$, withholding 2021), the same residual calculation gives $\hat\sigma^2\approx32.7\text{ cm}^2$, $\hat\sigma\approx5.72$ cm. Predicting the withheld year:

$$\hat{t} = 97.92\text{ cm}, \qquad 97.92 \pm 1.96(5.72) \;\approx\; (86.7,\ 109.1)\text{ cm}$$

The actual 2021 rainfall, 102 cm, falls comfortably inside this interval — a prediction interval that can actually be checked against reality, and passes. This is also where the earlier $SE_{\text{pred}}$ formula's extra term matters: 2021 ($u=5$) sits very close to the data's center, while 2026 ($u=10$) sits far past its edge, so the true (non-simplified) uncertainty band for 2021 would be noticeably narrower than for 2026 — interpolated predictions aren't just more trustworthy in principle, MLE's own machinery quantifies exactly how much more.

LS vs. MLE, in one line: LS picks a criterion and directly optimizes it. MLE starts one level deeper — with an assumption about how the noise behaves — and that assumption happens to hand you the same criterion LS was using all along, plus a noise estimate LS never had access to.

"If MLE Gives the Same $\hat{w}$, Why Bother With It At All?"

Fair question — and worth asking directly, since we just showed the two methods tie on the fitted line itself. The answer is that the probability model behind MLE gives you things pure LS structurally cannot, even when the fitted line matches:

  1. It explains why squared error is the right loss. LS just assumes it; MLE derives it from the Gaussian noise assumption. Change that assumption (e.g., to Laplace noise) and MLE derives a different loss instead — squared error was never a law, just a consequence of one particular noise assumption.
  2. It estimates the noise itself ($\hat{\sigma}^2$) — needed for confidence intervals, hypothesis tests on individual weights, and model-comparison scores (AIC/BIC), none of which plain LS can produce.
  3. It gives a full predictive distribution, not just a point. $N(\hat{w}x_{\text{new}}, \hat{\sigma}^2)$ instead of a bare number, so you can say "95% of the time the true value falls in this range."
  4. It makes regularization principled instead of ad hoc. Put a prior on $w$ (e.g., $w\sim N(0,\tau^2)$) and Ridge regression falls out as the MAP estimate, with $\lambda$ meaning something concrete ($\sigma^2/\tau^2$) instead of being an arbitrary knob.
  5. It's the exact same machine reused everywhere downstream. Logistic Regression, Gaussian Naive Bayes, GMM/EM, even the SFT cross-entropy loss on the LLM Engine page are all "assume a generative model → write the likelihood → maximize it." Learning MLE here, where it happens to reduce to something familiar, is what makes every later derivation feel like reapplication instead of a brand-new trick each time.

Regularization: Preventing Overfitting by Penalizing Large Weights

The passing note earlier warned that higher-order polynomials can drive training error to zero while producing wild, memorized-noise predictions between data points — overfitting. The most direct fix doesn't reduce the model's flexibility (its degree $M$) at all; instead it changes what counts as a good fit, by adding a penalty for large weights directly into the cost function being minimized. This is L2 (Ridge) regularization:

$$S_{\text{reg}}(w,c) = \sum_{n=1}^{N}(t_n - wx_n - c)^2 \;+\; \lambda w^2$$

The new term $\lambda w^2$ grows whenever $|w|$ grows, so the optimizer is no longer purely chasing the smallest residuals — it now trades residual size off against keeping $w$ small. $\lambda \ge 0$ controls how heavily that trade-off is weighted: $\lambda=0$ recovers plain Least Squares exactly, and larger $\lambda$ pulls $w$ progressively toward zero.

Why only $w$ gets penalized, not $c$: shrinking $c$ toward zero would make the model's predictions depend on an arbitrary choice — here, we coded years as $u=\text{year}-2016$, so $c$ is tied to that specific choice of origin. Had we instead coded from 2000 or 2020, $c$ would come out completely different even though the line itself is the same. Penalizing $w$ doesn't have this problem, since the slope is unaffected by where the origin is placed (as established earlier). Penalizing an arbitrary, origin-dependent number would be penalizing a modeling accident, not a genuine property of the fit.

Solving the Regularized Problem

Differentiating $S_{\text{reg}}$ with respect to $c$ is unaffected by the new term (it doesn't involve $c$), so $c=\bar{t}-w\bar{u}$ still holds exactly as before. Substituting that back in and writing everything in terms of centered variables $\tilde{u}_n = u_n-\bar{u}$, $\tilde{t}_n=t_n-\bar{t}$:

$$\frac{\partial S_{\text{reg}}}{\partial w} = -2\sum_n \tilde{u}_n(\tilde{t}_n - w\tilde{u}_n) + 2\lambda w = 0 \;\;\Rightarrow\;\; w_{\text{reg}} = \frac{\sum_n \tilde{u}_n\tilde{t}_n}{\sum_n \tilde{u}_n^2 + \lambda}$$

Compare this to the unregularized slope derived earlier, $w=\frac{\sum\tilde{u}_n\tilde{t}_n}{\sum\tilde{u}_n^2}$ — identical, except $\lambda$ has been added to the denominator. That single added term is the entire mechanism of L2 regularization: a bigger denominator means a smaller $w$, and $\lambda$ controls by how much.

Choosing $\lambda$: Where the Validation Set Comes In

The normal equations solve for $(w,c)$ directly, in one closed-form calculation — but only once $\lambda$ is already fixed. $\lambda$ itself sits outside that calculation entirely; it's a setting chosen before the normal equations ever run, not something they solve for. That's a genuinely different kind of decision than fitting $(w,c)$, and it needs its own data-driven procedure.

This is where a validation set comes back in — not to monitor a training curve the way it would for an iterative classifier (there isn't one here), but purely to select $\lambda$:

  1. Split the data into a training set and a validation set.
  2. For each candidate $\lambda$ (e.g., the $0, 50, 200,\dots$ from the table above), fit $(w,c)$ using only the training set.
  3. Evaluate that fitted model's error on the validation set — data it never touched while fitting.
  4. Pick whichever $\lambda$ gives the lowest validation error — never the lowest training error, which would trivially always favor $\lambda=0$, the same pitfall that made training error the wrong signal for choosing the polynomial degree $M$ earlier.

A separate test set, untouched by both the $(w,c)$ fitting and the $\lambda$ selection, is then used exactly once at the end for an honest final performance estimate.

With a small dataset — like the 10-point rainfall example — a single fixed validation split wastes too much data and can be noisy just from which points happened to land in which split. In practice, k-fold cross-validation is used instead: repeat the whole train-then-validate procedure $k$ times, rotating which slice of the data plays the validation role each time, then average the validation error across all $k$ rounds before comparing candidate $\lambda$ values.

Figure: In each of the $k$ rounds, one fold takes a turn as the validation set while the rest train the model; the final score for a candidate (here, a candidate $\lambda$) is the average validation error across all $k$ rounds — so every data point is used for validation exactly once, and for training in every other round.

The Probabilistic View: Regularization as MAP Estimation

Point 4 in the previous section mentioned this in passing — here's the connection made explicit. Put a Gaussian prior directly on the weight, $w\sim N(0,\tau^2)$, expressing a belief that $w$ is probably small unless the data strongly indicates otherwise. Combining this prior with the Gaussian-noise likelihood from earlier and maximizing the resulting posterior (MAP, not plain MLE) leads to exactly the regularized objective $S_{\text{reg}}$ above, with

$$\lambda = \frac{\sigma^2}{\tau^2}$$

This gives $\lambda$ real meaning instead of being a knob tuned by trial and error: it's the ratio of how noisy the data is ($\sigma^2$) to how strongly we believe $w$ should stay small ($\tau^2$). Noisy data or a strong small-weight belief both push $\lambda$ up; clean data or an indifferent prior ($\tau^2\to\infty$) push $\lambda\to0$, recovering plain Least Squares — the same MLE-with-a-prior pattern used throughout this series, now applied to the model's own parameters instead of its predictions.

Logistic Regression

Up to this point, our linear models have focused entirely on regression—predicting a continuous, unconstrained numerical quantity like rainfall or temperature. But what happens when the target variable isn't continuous, but a binary choice?

Classification is the task of predicting discrete categories—such as whether an email is spam ($1$) or not spam ($0$), or whether a medical tumor is malignant ($1$) or benign ($0$). If we try to naively apply standard Linear Regression $y = wx + c$ to a binary problem, the straight line will stretch continuously from $-\infty$ to $+\infty$, making it impossible to interpret the output as a valid probability between $0$ and $1$.

The solution is to keep our linear combination $z = wx + c$ (often called the log-odds or linear score), but pass it through a non-linear sigmoid function $\sigma(z)$ to "squeeze" the output into a strict range between $0$ and $1$:

$$\hat{y} = \sigma(wx + c) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(wx + c)}}$$

This single architectural tweak transforms our model from a geometric line fit into a probabilistic classifier.

Why Least Squares Fails Here (And MLE Takes Over)

In linear regression, minimizing squared error came naturally from a Gaussian noise assumption. But if you plug the non-linear sigmoid prediction into a squared error loss function $\sum (y_i - \sigma(wx_i + c))^2$, the resulting loss surface is no longer a clean bowl. Instead, it becomes non-convex, riddled with local minima where gradient-based optimization can easily get trapped.

To fix this, we switch from a Gaussian noise assumption to a Bernoulli trial assumption (coin flips with probability $\hat{y}$). Applying Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) to this Bernoulli setup discards the squared error and yields a completely different objective function: Binary Cross-Entropy Loss. This loss function turns the optimization landscape back into a smooth, bowl-like convex surface that gradient descent can reliably solve, even though it lacks the clean closed-form normal equations of linear regression.

Multinomial Logistic Regression / Softmax Regression (K Classes)

Derivation: Softmax + Categorical Cross-Entropy Gradient

This document proves why the derivative of the Cross-Entropy loss with respect to the input logits simplifies directly to the prediction error: \(\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{Y}\).

1. Definitions & Setup

Softmax Activation: For logits \(\mathbf{z} = [z_1, z_2, \dots, z_K]^\top\), the probability for class \(i\) is:

\[ P_i = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum_{j=1}^K e^{z_j}} \]

Categorical Cross-Entropy Loss: For a one-hot encoded vector \(\mathbf{y}\), the loss \(\mathcal{L}\) is:

\[ \mathcal{L} = -\sum_{k=1}^K y_k \ln(P_k) \]
2. Derivative of Softmax Output (\(\frac{\partial P_k}{\partial z_i}\))

Using the Quotient Rule \(\left(\frac{u}{v}\right)' = \frac{u'v - uv'}{v^2}\), we consider two cases:

Case 1: When \(k = i\) (Derivative with respect to its own logit):

\[ \frac{\partial P_i}{\partial z_i} = \frac{e^{z_i} \left(\sum e^{z_j}\right) - e^{z_i}(e^{z_i})}{\left(\sum e^{z_j}\right)^2} = \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum e^{z_j}} \left(1 - \frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum e^{z_j}}\right) = P_i(1 - P_i) \]

Case 2: When \(k \neq i\) (Derivative with respect to a different logit):

\[ \frac{\partial P_k}{\partial z_i} = \frac{0 \cdot \left(\sum e^{z_j}\right) - e^{z_k}(e^{z_i})}{\left(\sum e^{z_j}\right)^2} = -\left(\frac{e^{z_k}}{\sum e^{z_j}}\right) \left(\frac{e^{z_i}}{\sum e^{z_j}}\right) = -P_k P_i \]
3. Applying the Chain Rule to Loss (\(\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i}\))

Differentiating loss \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to logit \(z_i\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = -\sum_{k=1}^K \frac{y_k}{P_k} \frac{\partial P_k}{\partial z_i} \]

Splitting the sum into the \(k = i\) term and all \(k \neq i\) terms:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = -\left[ \frac{y_i}{P_i} \cdot \frac{\partial P_i}{\partial z_i} + \sum_{k \neq i}^K \frac{y_k}{P_k} \cdot \frac{\partial P_k}{\partial z_i} \right] \]

Substituting Case 1 and Case 2 derivatives:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = -\left[ \frac{y_i}{P_i} \cdot P_i(1 - P_i) + \sum_{k \neq i}^K \frac{y_k}{P_k} \cdot (-P_k P_i) \right] \]

Canceling out \(P_i\) and \(P_k\) in the numerators/denominators:

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = -y_i(1 - P_i) + \sum_{k \neq i}^K y_k P_i = -y_i + y_i P_i + \sum_{k \neq i}^K y_k P_i \]

Factoring out \(P_i\):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = -y_i + P_i \left( y_i + \sum_{k \neq i}^K y_k \right) = -y_i + P_i \left( \sum_{k=1}^K y_k \right) \]
4. Final Simplification

Since \(\mathbf{y}\) is a one-hot encoded vector, the sum of its components is always 1 (\(\sum_{k=1}^K y_k = 1\)):

\[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial z_i} = P_i - y_i \]

In vector form across all \(K\) classes:

\[ \mathbf{\nabla_{\mathbf{z}} \mathcal{L} = \mathbf{P} - \mathbf{Y}} \]
1. Binary Logistic Regression (2 Classes)

Uses a single weight vector w and bias c to map inputs to a probability p ∈ (0, 1) via the Sigmoid activation function.

Algorithm: Binary Training via Gradient Descent
  1. Initialize: Set weight w = 0, bias c = 0, learning rate η.
  2. Loop for each training epoch:
    1. Reset gradient accumulators: grad_w = 0, grad_c = 0.
    2. For each sample (x, y) in dataset:
      • Compute linear score: z = w * x + c
      • Predict probability: p = 1 / (1 + e-z)
      • Accumulate gradients: grad_w += (p - y) * x, grad_c += (p - y)
    3. Update parameters: w = w - η * grad_w, c = c - η * grad_c
  3. Output: Learned boundary parameter w and bias c.
2. Multinomial Logistic Regression / Softmax Regression (K Classes)

Generalizes binary classification to K > 2 mutually exclusive classes. Maintains a distinct weight vector wk and bias ck for every class, normalized into a probability distribution via Softmax.

Algorithm: Multiclass Training via Softmax
  1. Initialize: Set weight matrix W (size K x features) and bias vector C to 0. Set learning rate η.
  2. Loop for each training epoch:
    1. Reset gradient accumulators grad_W = 0 and grad_C = 0.
    2. For each sample (x, y_onehot) in dataset:
      • Compute linear score vector for all K classes: Z = W * x + C
      • Compute Softmax probabilities: Pk = eZk / Σ (eZj) for all classes j
      • Compute error vector: error = P - y_onehot
      • Accumulate gradients for each class vector k: grad_Wk += errork * x, grad_Ck += errork
    3. Update matrix: W = W - η * grad_W, C = C - η * grad_C
  3. Output: Learned parameter set W and C for all K classes.

Bias and Variance

Bias and Variance are not properties of a single fitted line — they're properties of a fitting procedure, examined by asking a hypothetical question: if this same procedure were repeated on a different sample of training data, how would the result change? This applies across Linear models broadly — Linear Regression, Logistic Regression, Ridge Regression, and Discriminant methods are all fitting procedures that this same question can be asked of, even though each one fits something different (a line, a decision boundary, a set of class scores).

Steps to Compute Bias and Variance

  1. Draw many different training datasets. $D_1, D_2, \dots, D_n$, each sampled independently from the same underlying data-generating process (in the Linear Regression case: the same true rainfall-vs-year relationship, with fresh random noise each time).
  2. Fit the model separately on each dataset. Run the full fitting procedure — for Linear Regression, this means Steps 1–3 from the numerical example (construct the normal equations, differentiate, solve) — once per dataset, completely independently. This produces $n$ different fitted lines, $\hat f_{D_1}, \hat f_{D_2}, \dots, \hat f_{D_n}$, each with its own $(w,c)$.
  3. Pick one fixed test point $x_0$. This point does not change across the rest of the steps — only the training dataset (and therefore the fitted line) changes.
  4. Evaluate every fitted line at that same $x_0$. This gives $n$ predictions for the same point: $\hat f_{D_1}(x_0), \hat f_{D_2}(x_0), \dots, \hat f_{D_n}(x_0)$ — one number from each of the $n$ separately-fitted lines.
  5. Average those $n$ predictions. $$\bar{f}(x_0) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \hat f_{D_i}(x_0)$$ This average is what all those independently-fitted lines agree on, on average, at $x_0$.
  6. Compute Bias at $x_0$ — how far that average sits from the true value: $$\text{Bias}(x_0) = \bar{f}(x_0) - f(x_0)$$ (In practice, $\text{Bias}(x_0)^2$ is what's reported, matching the decomposition formula.)
  7. Compute Variance at $x_0$ — how spread out the $n$ individual predictions are from their own average (the true value $f(x_0)$ never enters this calculation): $$\text{Variance}(x_0) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\big(\hat f_{D_i}(x_0) - \bar{f}(x_0)\big)^2$$

For Linear Regression specifically: Step 2 means many separate straight lines get determined — not one line evaluated at many $x$'s, but many independently-fitted lines, each from its own resampled dataset. Step 5 is the average line's prediction at $x_0$; Step 7's variance is how much the individual lines' predictions at that one point disagree with each other, not with the truth.

The Real-World Picture: Archery

Imagine several archers, each shooting a group of arrows at a target, where the bullseye is the true relationship you're trying to model:

  • Low bias, low variance: arrows cluster tightly, right on the bullseye — the good fit.
  • High bias, low variance: arrows cluster tightly together, but the whole group is off-center — consistent, but consistently wrong.
  • Low bias, high variance: arrows scatter widely, but their average position is near the bullseye — individually unreliable, even though there's no consistent direction of error.
  • High bias, high variance: arrows scatter widely and the group is off-center — the worst of both problems at once.

Figure: Each blue dot is $\hat f_{D_i}(x_0)$ — one model's prediction at the same fixed point $x_0$, from a line fitted on a different dataset $D_i$. The red bullseye is $f(x_0)$, the true value. Spread among the dots is variance; distance of the group's center from the bullseye is bias.

Back in the rainfall setting already used on this page: a degree-1 line ($M=1$) is the high-bias, low-variance corner — it barely changes even if you drop or add a data point (low variance), but if the true rainfall trend actually curves, a straight line can never capture that no matter how much data you feed it (high bias). A degree-9 polynomial through the same 10 points is the low-bias, high-variance corner — flexible enough to fit any underlying trend on average, but so sensitive to the specific sample that removing or changing even one point (as in the 2021 held-out example above) would swing the fitted curve wildly. Regularization (the $\lambda$ term from earlier) works by deliberately trading a little bias for a large reduction in variance — shrinking an overly flexible model's weights back toward the reliable-but-simpler end of this spectrum.
