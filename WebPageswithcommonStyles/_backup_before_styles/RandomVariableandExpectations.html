Probability Distributions

Random variable

A real valued function defined over a sample space of random experiment is called the random variable. A variable which takes corresponding to the outcomes of a random experiment is called random variable.

eg (1) In the experiment of throwing a die, the outcomes are 1,2,3,4,5,6 so the random variable of getting a number takes values 1,2,3,4,5,6

(2) In the case of tossing 3 coins the outcome are getting 0 head, 1 head, 2 heads and 3 head. So the random variable of getting heads takes the value 0,1,2,3.

Probability Distribution (Distribution of random variables)

Let 'X' be a random variable. Let X assume values as $x_1, x_2, x_3 \dots$

The probability that X takes any x ($x_1$ or $x_2$ or $x_3 \dots$) is given by $P(X = x)$. It can also be denoted by $f(x)$.

Probability Density is the distribution of probabilities with respect to its random variable. These distributions are classified into two main types:

1. Discrete Distribution

Takes on individual, countable values (e.g., 0, 1, 2 heads). Probabilities are assigned to each specific point.

Formula: $\sum P(x) = 1$

2. Continuous Distribution

Takes on values within a continuous range (e.g., height, temperature, time). Probability is measured over an interval.

Formula: $\int f(x)dx = 1$

Key Difference: For Discrete variables, we ask: "What is the probability of exactly 2?" For Continuous variables, the probability of an exact single point is technically zero; instead, we ask: "What is the probability of a value falling between 1.9 and 2.1?"

For example, tossing two fair coins (note: this is a separate, simpler case than eg(2) above, which used three coins):
$x = 0,1,2$ indicates no head, one head, two heads, so that $P(X = 0) = 1/4, P(X = 1) = 1/2, P(X = 2) = 1/4$. Check: $1/4 + 1/2 + 1/4 = 1$, as required for any probability distribution.
So the probability distribution of x (getting heads) is:

X P(X)
0 1/4
1 1/2
2 1/4

Example 2: Two Dice Rolls

When rolling two six-sided dice, the joint distribution shows the probability of every possible pair (e.g., Die 1 = 2 and Die 2 = 5). Since each die is independent, every combination has a probability of $1/6 \times 1/6 = 1/36$.

Expectations and Covariances

There are two operations involving probability distribution: (1) Expectations and (2) Covariances.

1. Expectation: The sum of the products of the random variable values and their probabilities.

For a discrete distribution: $$E[f] = \sum f(x) \cdot p(x)$$ where f(x) is the random variable and p(x) is the corresponding probability

Example: Using the sum-of-two-dice distribution from Figure 2 above, here is a partial sum over just the outcomes where the sum is less than 7 — useful as an arithmetic exercise, though note this is not the same as the conditional expectation $E[\text{Sum} \mid \text{Sum}<7]$ (see the note below):

(2 × 0.028) + (3 × 0.056) + (4 × 0.083) + (5 × 0.111) + (6 × 0.139)
= 0.056 + 0.168 + 0.332 + 0.555 + 0.834 = 1.945
Why isn't 1.945 the conditional expectation? A true conditional expectation $E[\text{Sum}\mid\text{Sum}<7]$ requires dividing this sum by $P(\text{Sum}<7)$ — the total probability mass of the outcomes being summed — not just adding the $x \cdot p(x)$ terms directly. Here, $P(\text{Sum}<7) = 0.028+0.056+0.083+0.111+0.139 = 0.417$, so: $$E[\text{Sum}\mid\text{Sum}<7] = \frac{1.945}{0.417} \approx 4.665$$ Without that division, 1.945 is just the raw contribution of those five outcomes to the overall (unconditional) expectation $E[\text{Sum}]=7$ — a valid quantity, but a different one from the conditional expectation.
2. Variance: The variance of a probability distribution is defined by: $$Var[f] = E[(f(x) - E[f])^2]$$

It provides a measure of how much variability there is in the values of $f(x)$ around its mean $E[f]$.

For an interactive, visual sense of how variance controls the spread of a distribution, see the $\mu, \sigma^2$ slider widget on the Normal Distribution page — dragging the variance slider there shows exactly this spreading-out effect in real time.

3. Covariance:Covariance involves variances between two or random variables For two random variables x and y, the covariance measures the extent to which the two variables vary together and is defined by

$$cov[x; y] = E[[x − E[x]][y − E[y]]]= E[xy] − E[x]E[y]$$

Generalizing to $n$ Dimensions: The Covariance Matrix

The formula above covers exactly one pair of variables, $x$ and $y$. Most real data isn't a single pair — it's a random vector of $n$ variables at once, $\mathbf{X} = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n)^T$ (e.g., a patient's height, weight, and blood pressure together, or the $n$ pixel values of an image). Covariance generalizes to this case as the Covariance Matrix, usually written $\Sigma$ — the same $\Sigma$ used in the Multivariate Normal Distribution on the Normal Distribution page.

Definition: for a random vector $\mathbf{X}=(x_1,\dots,x_n)^T$ with mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}=(\mu_1,\dots,\mu_n)^T$, the covariance matrix is: $$\Sigma = E\big[(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})(\mathbf{X}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T\big]$$

Written out element by element, entry $(i,j)$ of $\Sigma$ is just the two-variable covariance formula applied to $x_i$ and $x_j$:

$$\Sigma_{ij} = \text{cov}[x_i, x_j] = E[(x_i-\mu_i)(x_j-\mu_j)]$$
Size of the output: for $n$ variables, $\Sigma$ is always an $n \times n$ matrix — one row and one column per variable. So 2 variables give a $2\times2$ matrix, 3 variables give $3\times3$, and in general $n$ variables give $n\times n$, regardless of how many data points ($N$) you have. Two structural facts fall directly out of the definition:
  • The diagonal holds variances, not covariances. When $i=j$, $\Sigma_{ii}=E[(x_i-\mu_i)^2]=\text{Var}[x_i]$ — the ordinary single-variable variance you already know, just sitting on the diagonal.
  • $\Sigma$ is symmetric. Since $\text{cov}[x_i,x_j]=\text{cov}[x_j,x_i]$ (swapping the order inside the expectation changes nothing), $\Sigma_{ij}=\Sigma_{ji}$ for every pair — the matrix is a mirror image of itself across the diagonal.

Worked example, $n=3$: for variables $x_1, x_2, x_3$, the $3\times3$ covariance matrix is:

$$\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} \text{Var}[x_1] & \text{cov}[x_1,x_2] & \text{cov}[x_1,x_3] \\ \text{cov}[x_2,x_1] & \text{Var}[x_2] & \text{cov}[x_2,x_3] \\ \text{cov}[x_3,x_1] & \text{cov}[x_3,x_2] & \text{Var}[x_3] \end{bmatrix}$$

Figure: Layout of a $3\times3$ covariance matrix. Note the mirror symmetry — the cell at row $i$, column $j$ always matches the cell at row $j$, column $i$, since $\text{cov}[x_i,x_j]=\text{cov}[x_j,x_i]$.

Key Probability Distributions

Probability distributions are categorized based on whether the random variable is Discrete (countable values) or Continuous (any value within a range). While the Normal Distribution (Gaussian) is the most famous, it is part of a broader family of distributions used to model different types of data.

1. Continuous Distributions

  • Normal (Gaussian) Distribution: The "Bell Curve." It is defined by its mean ($\mu$) and standard deviation ($\sigma$). It is the cornerstone of the Central Limit Theorem.
  • Uniform Distribution (Continuous): Every value in a specific range $[a, b]$ has an equal probability of occurring.

2. Discrete Distributions

  • Binomial Distribution: Models the number of "successes" in a fixed number of independent trials (e.g., flipping a coin 10 times).
  • Poisson Distribution: Models the number of events occurring within a fixed interval of time or space (e.g., how many customers arrive at a shop in an hour).
Key Point: Although these distributions are distinct, the Law of Large Numbers and the Central Limit Theorem show that both Binomial and Poisson distributions start to look like a Normal Distribution as the number of trials or events increases.
