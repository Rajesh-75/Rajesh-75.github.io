Normal (Gaussian) Distributions

The Normal Distribution, also known as the Gaussian Distribution, is a widely used continuous probability distribution. Its fundamental importance lies in the fact that many natural populations follow a pattern that approximates the Normal Curve. Numerous phenomena—such as the age distribution of a specific animal species, the heights of adults, and various industrial measurements—are considered to be normally distributed. Normal distribution has an important feature that many probability distributions can be approximated to Normal distributions when the number of random variables becomes very large. As in the case of any probability distributions, Gaussian distribution gives a probability for a given random variable.

Manufacturing Example:

Consider a company manufacturing screws of a specific diameter. While the machinery is precise, there will always be slight variations in measurement. Most screws will have a diameter very close to the target; however, a few will be slightly larger or smaller.

If we plot a histogram of these measurements, the highest frequency occurs at the center (the mean). As we move away from the center, the frequency decreases symmetrically on both sides. By reducing the class intervals to be infinitely small, the histogram transforms into a smooth, bell-shaped curve. This is the Normal Curve. By dividing the frequency by the total number of observations, the distribution is normalized to represent probability, where the total area under the curve is exactly 1. The ordinate at mean divides the whole part into two parts. The area to the right of the ordinate and that of left of the ordinate are both 0.5.

Mathematical Definition of Normal Distribution

A continuous random variable $X$ is said to follow a Normal Distribution (Gaussian distribution) if its probability density function (PDF) is defined by the following equation:

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sigma \sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma} \right)^2}$$

Where the parameters are defined as:

  • $\mu$ (Mu): The Mean or center of the distribution.
  • $\sigma$ (Sigma): The Standard Deviation, representing the spread.
  • A random variable $X$ following a Normal Distribution is typically represented as $X \sim N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, where $\mu$ is the mean and $\sigma^2$ is the variance.
  • $e$: The base of natural logarithms (approx. 2.718).
  • $\pi$: The constant Pi (approx. 3.1415).
  • The curve is asymptotic to the base on either side; i.e., the curve approaches nearer and nearer to the base, but never touches.
  • The points of inflection occur at $\mu \pm \sigma$.
  • Area Distribution (The Empirical Rule):
    • $\mu \pm 1\sigma$ covers 68.27% of the area.
    • $\mu \pm 2\sigma$ covers 95.45% of the area.
    • $\mu \pm 3\sigma$ covers 99.73% of the area.
  • Additive Property: A linear function of normal variates is also a normal variate. This property ensures that the sum or difference of independent normally distributed variables results in a new variable that is also normally distributed.

The variable $X$ is defined over the theoretical range $-\infty < X < +\infty$. However, for most practical applications, the range is considered to be $\mu - 3\sigma$ to $\mu + 3\sigma$, as it encompasses nearly all possible outcomes. In this context, $X$ is treated as a Continuous Random Variable representing a single measurement, allowing us to calculate the probability of $X$ falling within any given interval under the Gaussian curve.

Standardized Normal Distribution

If X is a random variable following a normal distribution with mean $\mu$ and standard deviation $\sigma$, then the variable $Z$ = $\frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$ is known as the Standard Normal Variable. It is usually represented as $Z \sim N(0,1)$, where 0 is the mean and 1 is the variance of the standard Normal Distribution.

When we standardize the distribution ($ \mu=0, \sigma=1 $), the complex Gaussian equation simplifies significantly. This is the version used to calculate Z-scores and probability tables:The table is used to compute the area within two values of any variable following ND.

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-\frac{x^2}{2}}$$

In this form, the total area under the curve is exactly 1, and the curve is perfectly centered at zero. The Z-Score table is useful to compute the area between two values of any variable following the Standard Normal Distribution.

For example, when $Z = 1.17$, the table value is $0.3790$. This represents the area between zero and $1.17$, written as:
$P(0 < Z < 1.17) = 0.3790$

Because the curve is symmetrical, the area is the same for both negative and positive values of $Z$. That is: $P(-1.17 < Z < 0) = P(0 < Z < 1.17) = 0.3790$.

If neither value is zero, for example $P(-1.78 < Z < 1.78)$, we must calculate the area from each point to zero and then sum them (if they are on opposite sides of the mean):

$P(-1.78 < Z < 1.78) = P(-1.78 < Z < 0) + P(0 < Z < 1.78)$
$= 0.4625 + 0.4625 = 0.9250$
$P(Z > 1.8) = P(0 < Z < \infty) - P(0 < Z < 1.8)$
$P(Z > 1.8) = 0.5000 - 0.4641 = 0.0359$
$P(Z > 1.8) = 0.5000 - 0.4641 = 0.0359$
$P(Z < 1.2) = 0.5000 + 0.3849 = 0.8849$

Key Rule for Calculations: To find the total area relative to a positive $Z$-value, consider the two halves of the curve. To compute the area less than a positive $Z$ (e.g., $P(Z < 1.2)$), you take the entire left half $P(-\infty < Z < 0) = 0.5$ and add the area between zero and your value. Conversely, to compute the area greater than a positive $Z$ (the upper tail), you take the entire right half $P(0 < Z < \infty) = 0.5$ and subtract the area between zero and your value.

Detailed Calculation Example:

A Random Variable $X$ follows a normal distribution with mean $\mu = 45$ and standard deviation $\sigma = 10$. Find the probability for an item to fall: (1) Beyond 60 and (2) Between 40 and 56.

(i) Probability beyond 60 ($x > 60$)

Convert to $Z$: $z = \frac{60 - 45}{10} = 1.5$.
$P(x > 60) = P(z > 1.5) = 0.5000 - P(0 < z < 1.5) = 0.5000 - 0.4332 = 0.0668$.

(ii) Probability between 40 and 56 ($40 < x < 56$)

Convert boundaries: $z_1 = \frac{40-45}{10} = -0.5$ and $z_2 = \frac{56-45}{10} = 1.1$.
$P(40 < x < 56) = P(-0.5 < z < 1.1) = 0.1915 + 0.3643 = 0.5558$.

Another real life example :

In a competitive examination, 5000 students appeared for a paper in Statistics (let's say, a GATE exam). The average mark was 62 and the standard deviation was 12. If there are only 100 vacancies (rankings), find the minimum mark that one should secure to get selected against a vacancy.

$\mu = 62$ and $\sigma = 12$
There are $100$ vacancies out of $5,000$ students, i.e., $\frac{100}{5000} \times 100 = 2\%$.
The probability of selection is $0.02$.
$\therefore P(Z > z_1) = 0.02$
The area from the mean to $z_1$ is $P(0 < Z < z_1) = 0.5000 - 0.02 = 0.4800$.
From the Z-Table, the value for $0.4800$ is $z = 2.05$.
$$Z = \frac{x - \mu}{\sigma}$$ $$2.05 = \frac{x - 62}{12}$$ $$x - 62 = 12 \times 2.05$$ $$x = 24.6 + 62 = 86.6$$ Conclusion: Therefore, the minimum mark required is 86.6.

Proof for The Additive Property

A significant characteristic of the Normal Distribution is that it is stable under linear combinations. This means that if you add two independent normally distributed random variables, the resulting variable is also normally distributed.

Mathematical Proof Sketch:

Given independent $X \sim N(\mu_X, \sigma_X^2)$ and $Y \sim N(\mu_Y, \sigma_Y^2)$, let $Z = X + Y$. Using Moment Generating Functions (MGF):

$M_Z(t) = M_X(t) \cdot M_Y(t)$
$M_Z(t) = e^{\mu_X t + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_X^2 t^2} \cdot e^{\mu_Y t + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_Y^2 t^2}$
$M_Z(t) = e^{(\mu_X + \mu_Y)t + \frac{1}{2}(\sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2)t^2}$

Because the result is in the form $e^{\mu_{new} t + \frac{1}{2}\sigma_{new}^2 t^2}$, we conclude that $Z \sim N(\mu_X + \mu_Y, \sigma_X^2 + \sigma_Y^2)$.

Multivariate Normal Distributions

1. Key Components: Mean Vector and Covariance Matrix

In multiple dimensions, the distribution is characterized by two primary structures that generalize the univariate mean and variance.

The Mean Vector ($\boldsymbol{\mu}$)

It is perfectly fine to say $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ denotes the Mean, but it is more precise to call it the Mean Vector. In one dimension, $\mu$ is a single number. In a multivariate distribution, $\boldsymbol{\mu}$ is a vector containing the means of every individual variable:

$$\boldsymbol{\mu} = \begin{bmatrix} \mu_1 \\ \mu_2 \\ \vdots \\ \mu_n \end{bmatrix}$$
The Covariance Matrix ($\Sigma$)

It is incorrect to call $\Sigma$ (or its inverse) the "Standard Deviation."

  • $\Sigma$ is the Covariance Matrix: It contains the variances of each variable on the diagonal and the covariances between pairs of variables on the off-diagonals.
  • Standard Deviation vs. Variance: In 1D, standard deviation is $\sigma$ and variance is $\sigma^2$. In multivariate math, $\Sigma$ is the "matrix version" of variance ($s^2$), not standard deviation ($s$).

2. The Mathematical Foundation

The PDF of a bivariate normal distribution (where $N=2$) is given by:

$$f(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{1}{2\pi \sqrt{|\Sigma|}} \exp\left( -\frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) \right)$$

For the density $f(\mathbf{x})$ to be a constant (a contour), the exponent must be constant:

$$(\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = c^2$$

This quadratic form is the equation of an ellipse. Therefore, the contours of a Multivariate Normal Distribution are always concentric ellipses centered at the mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$.

3. What the Contours Tell You

The shape and orientation of these ellipses are entirely determined by the Covariance Matrix ($\Sigma$):

Circle

If the variables are independent and have equal variance ($\sigma_1 = \sigma_2$ and $\rho = 0$), the contours are perfect circles.

Axis-Aligned Ellipse

If the variables are independent but have different variances, the ellipses stretch along the $x$ or $y$ axis.

Tilted Ellipse

If there is a correlation ($\rho \neq 0$) between variables, the ellipse tilts. The angle represents the strength and direction of correlation.

4. Connection to Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

To physically "derive" or draw the plot, mathematicians look at the Spectral Decomposition of the covariance matrix $\Sigma$:

  • Eigenvectors: Define the direction of the principal axes (the "tilt") of the ellipse.
  • Eigenvalues ($\lambda$): Define the lengths of the axes. The width of the contour in the direction of an eigenvector is proportional to $\sqrt{\lambda}$.

5. Why this matters in Neyman-Pearson

Returning to our earlier discussion on Neyman-Pearson, if you are testing two multivariate hypotheses:

$H_0: \mathbf{x} \sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_0, \Sigma)$    $H_1: \mathbf{x} \sim N(\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \Sigma)$

The "Likelihood Ratio" effectively compares two sets of these contour ellipses. The Optimal Decision Boundary where the ratio is constant ($k$) will actually be a straight line (or hyperplane in higher dimensions) that cuts through the intersection of these overlapping ellipses.

6. High-Dimensional "ND" (N > 2)

If you have more than two dimensions (ND), you cannot visualize the full contour in a single 2D plot. Instead, you use:

  • Slice: Fix $N-2$ variables at their mean and plot the remaining two.
  • Projection: Project the $N$-dimensional "hyper-ellipsoid" onto a 2D plane.
  • Pair Plots: A grid of 2D contour plots showing every possible pair of variables.

Normal Distribution in Machine Learning & Deep Learning

The Gaussian shows up throughout ML and DL for two reinforcing reasons: the Central Limit Theorem means aggregated noise and averaged quantities genuinely tend toward it, and its exponential-quadratic form is mathematically convenient — log-likelihoods become simple quadratics, and many operations (sums, marginals, conditionals) stay Gaussian. The sections below trace that convenience through classical ML, deep learning, and the statistical machinery underneath both.

Statistical Foundations

MLE ↔ Least Squares

Assume regression targets $y_i = f(x_i;\theta) + \epsilon_i$ with noise $\epsilon_i \sim N(0,\sigma^2)$. The log-likelihood is $$\ell(\theta) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_i (y_i - f(x_i;\theta))^2$$ Maximizing $\ell(\theta)$ over $\theta$ is identical to minimizing $\sum_i (y_i-f(x_i;\theta))^2$ — ordinary least squares is Gaussian-noise MLE in disguise.

CLT & Confidence Intervals

By the Central Limit Theorem, a sample mean $\bar{X}_n$ approaches $N(\mu, \sigma^2/n)$ as $n$ grows, regardless of the underlying population. This is why an average metric across CV folds (accuracy, loss) is treated as approximately normal, justifying confidence intervals of the form $\bar{X} \pm z^{*}\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}$.

Classical Machine Learning

Gaussian Naive Bayes

This follows exactly the density-to-classification route you saw in the K-NN tutorial — model each class-conditional feature density and combine with Bayes' theorem — except here $p(x_j \mid C_k)$ is assumed Gaussian, $N(x_j; \mu_{jk}, \sigma_{jk}^2)$, instead of estimated via K-NN density estimation. Under the naive independence assumption, $p(\mathbf{x} \mid C_k) = \prod_j p(x_j \mid C_k)$, and classification follows $p(C_k \mid \mathbf{x}) \propto p(C_k)\prod_j p(x_j \mid C_k)$.

Anomaly Detection & Mahalanobis Distance

Fit $N(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \Sigma)$ to "normal" data, then flag points with low density as anomalies. The natural distance metric here is the Mahalanobis distance $$D_M(\mathbf{x}) = \sqrt{(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})^T \Sigma^{-1} (\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol{\mu})}$$ — literally the square root of the quadratic form $c^2$ from the multivariate section above. Contours of constant $D_M$ are exactly the concentric ellipses you already saw; a point far outside the inner ellipses is a candidate anomaly.

Deep Dive: The EM (Expectation-Maximization) Algorithm, via Gaussian Mixture Models

You've likely already seen K-means: pick $K$ centroids, assign each point to its nearest centroid (a hard, all-or-nothing assignment), recompute each centroid as the mean of its assigned points, and repeat. It's simple and fast, but has two real limits: every cluster is forced to be round and roughly the same size (since it only uses Euclidean distance to a centroid), and every point must commit 100% to exactly one cluster, even when it genuinely sits between two.However,K-Means makes an arbitrary choice, which can lead to poor results in noisy datasets.

EM generalizes exactly these two limits. Each cluster stops being a single point (a centroid) and becomes a full Gaussian — a mean and a covariance, so it can be circular, elongated, or tilted. And each data point no longer gets one hard label; it gets a soft(soft means You have a 70% chance of belonging to Group A, a 25% chance of Group B, and a 5% chance of Group C." (Probabilistic degrees of membership.In contrast,hard means you belong entirely to Group A. End of story." (Binary: Yes or No, 1 or 0). ), probabilistic degree of membership across every cluster. This generalized model — a weighted mixture of Gaussians instead of a set of centroids — is called a Gaussian Mixture Model (GMM), and EM is the algorithm that fits it, alternating between updating the soft memberships and updating the Gaussians until both settle down.By modeling data as a mixture of Gaussians, GMMs provide "soft" assignments. For every point, the model calculates the probability that it belongs to cluster A, cluster B, etc. This is far more realistic for data that has overlapping boundaries.For example,Point $i$ is assigned to cluster $k$ because cluster $k$ gives it the highest responsibility (or posterior probability), while the other clusters give it low responsibilities. By adjusting the covariance, the model can "stretch" to fit clusters that are elliptical or oriented diagonally, capturing structural patterns that K-Means would ignore.

A GMM models the data density as a weighted sum of $K$ Gaussians:

$$p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^{K} \pi_k \, N(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k), \qquad \sum_k \pi_k = 1$$.

Notation used above and in the E-step / M-step below:

  • $K$: the number of Gaussian components you choose to fit (e.g., the number of clusters you expect in the data).
  • $\pi_k$: the mixing weight of component $k$ — the overall proportion of the data belonging to that component, with $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$.
  • $N(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k)$: the multivariate Gaussian density for component $k$ (same $N(\cdot)$ from earlier on this page), with its own mean vector $\boldsymbol{\mu}_k$ and covariance matrix $\Sigma_k$.
  • $\mathbf{x}_i$: the $i$-th data point (a vector — e.g. 2D or higher-dimensional, following the bold vector notation from the Multivariate section above).
  • $\gamma_{ik}$: the responsibility — the probability that point $\mathbf{x}_i$ was generated by component $k$, given the current parameter estimates. For a fixed $i$, the responsibilities across all $k$ sum to 1.
  • $N_k$: the effective (soft) count of points assigned to component $k$ — the sum of $\mathbf{x}_i$'s responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$ across every data point. Unlike a hard count, this is usually not a whole number.
  • $N$: the total number of data points, used in $\pi_k = N_k/N$ to turn the soft count back into a proportion.
  • $T$ (in $(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^T$): matrix transpose — this outer product of a vector with itself is what builds up the covariance matrix, exactly as in the Multivariate section's $\Sigma$.

Figure: EM alternates between the E-step and M-step. Each pass is guaranteed to never decrease the data log-likelihood, so the loop provably settles down rather than oscillating forever.

Input: the raw data points $\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_N$ (unlabeled — no class information) and a chosen number of components $K$, plus some initial guess for $\pi_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k$ (often from a quick K-means run, as in the diagram above).

Output: the fitted parameters $\pi_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k$ for every component, and the responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$ — a soft, probabilistic cluster assignment for every point, rather than one hard label per point.

The two steps in the diagram, written out:

E-step — compute the responsibility of component $k$ for point $\mathbf{x}_i$, which is just Bayes' theorem again:

$$\gamma_{ik} = \frac{\pi_k \, N(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j \pi_j \, N(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol{\mu}_j,\Sigma_j)}$$

$\gamma_{ik}$ is normalized by construction: dividing by the sum over every component $j$ forces $\sum_k \gamma_{ik} = 1$ for each point $i$, so it's a genuine posterior probability, $P(\text{component } k \mid \mathbf{x}_i)$, not just a raw score. Matching each piece of the formula to Bayes' theorem, $P(k\mid x) = \frac{P(k)\,P(x\mid k)}{P(x)}$:

Bayes' theorem term In the GMM formula Meaning
Prior, $P(k)$ $\pi_k$ Before seeing the point, how likely is component $k$ overall (its mixing weight)
Likelihood, $P(x\mid k)$ $N(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol{\mu}_k,\Sigma_k)$ If the point did come from component $k$, how probable is a value like this
Evidence, $P(x)$ $\sum_j \pi_j N(\mathbf{x}_i;\boldsymbol{\mu}_j,\Sigma_j)$ Total probability of seeing this point at all, marginalizing over every component — the normalizer
Posterior, $P(k\mid x)$ $\gamma_{ik}$ After seeing the point, the updated belief that it came from component $k$

M-step — re-estimate each component using the responsibilities as soft weights:

$$N_k = \sum_i \gamma_{ik}, \quad \boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}\mathbf{x}_i, \quad \Sigma_k = \frac{1}{N_k}\sum_i \gamma_{ik}(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)(\mathbf{x}_i-\boldsymbol{\mu}_k)^T, \quad \pi_k = \frac{N_k}{N}$$

This is where the K-means connection from the top of this box becomes exact, not just a loose analogy: K-means is the limiting case of this same EM procedure as every $\Sigma_k \to$ a shared, vanishing isotropic covariance. In that limit, the soft responsibilities $\gamma_{ik}$ collapse to hard 0/1 nearest-centroid assignments, and the M-step update above becomes exactly the K-means centroid update. GMM/EM is the strictly more general algorithm; K-means is what you get by throwing away the covariance and the soft assignment.

From fitted parameters to an actual clustering: notice EM's output above is $\pi_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k$ and the responsibility matrix $\gamma_{ik}$ — not a single cluster label per point. Getting from there to clusters takes one more step, and there are two ways to take it:

  • Hard assignment (the usual choice): assign each point $\mathbf{x}_i$ to whichever component has the highest responsibility, $\underset{k}{\arg\max}\ \gamma_{ik}$. This is called the MAP (Maximum a Posteriori) assignment — you're picking the single most probable component instead of keeping the full probability spread. This is what you'd use to produce a K-means-style cluster label per point.
  • Keep the soft assignment: for points near a cluster boundary, forcing a single label throws away real information — a point with $\gamma_{i1}=0.51,\ \gamma_{i2}=0.49$ is genuinely ambiguous, and collapsing it to "cluster 1" hides that. Many downstream uses (e.g. flagging uncertain points, or feeding soft memberships into another model) keep the full $\gamma_{ik}$ vector instead of a hard label.
  • Assigning a brand-new point: for a point that wasn't in the training data, no retraining is needed — just plug it into the E-step formula above with the already-fitted $\pi_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \Sigma_k$ to get its responsibilities, then take the argmax if a hard label is required. This is exactly how a fitted GMM makes predictions on new data.

One more thing EM does not decide for you: the value of $K$ itself. Since adding more components can never decrease the training log-likelihood, picking $K$ by likelihood alone always favors more clusters — overfitting, in the limit, all the way to one Gaussian per point. In practice $K$ is chosen with a penalized criterion like BIC or AIC (which subtract a complexity penalty for extra parameters), or by held-out log-likelihood on validation data.

Deep Learning

Weight Initialization

Xavier/Glorot initialization draws weights from $N\!\left(0, \frac{2}{\text{fan\_in}+\text{fan\_out}}\right)$; He initialization (for ReLU networks) uses $N\!\left(0, \frac{2}{\text{fan\_in}}\right)$. Both choose the variance to keep activation and gradient magnitudes roughly stable across layers, preventing vanishing/exploding signals.

fan_in = number of input connections into the layer (forward-pass activation variance depends on this); fan_out = number of output connections leaving it (backward-pass gradient variance depends on this). Xavier averages both since it balances forward and backward signal; He uses only fan_in, with an extra factor of 2 to offset ReLU zeroing out roughly half of all activations.

Batch / Layer Normalization

For each mini-batch, activations are standardized exactly like the $Z$-transform earlier on this page: $\hat{x} = \frac{x - \mu_{\text{batch}}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{\text{batch}}^2+\epsilon}}$, then rescaled with learned parameters $y = \gamma\hat{x}+\beta$ to keep flexibility while stabilizing optimization.

Variational Autoencoders

The encoder outputs $\mu(x), \sigma(x)$ for an approximate posterior $q(z\mid x) = N(z; \mu(x), \sigma(x)^2 I)$. The reparameterization trick $z = \mu(x) + \sigma(x)\odot\epsilon$, $\epsilon\sim N(0,I)$, makes sampling differentiable, while a KL term pulls $q(z\mid x)$ toward the prior $N(0,I)$.

Diffusion Models

The forward process repeatedly adds small Gaussian noise, $x_t = \sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1} + \sqrt{\beta_t}\,\epsilon_t$, until the data becomes pure $N(0,I)$ noise. The network is trained to reverse this — predicting and removing noise at each step to walk back from noise to data.
