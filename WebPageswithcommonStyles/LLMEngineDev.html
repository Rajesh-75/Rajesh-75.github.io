Building an LLM Engine

From a Transformer on paper to a model that can be trained, aligned, and served — an engineering roadmap for what sits underneath a working LLM.

Where We Are

The previous pages in this series built the Transformer up from first principles: self-attention, the Encoder-only / Decoder-only / Encoder–Decoder architecture family, and a full worked Encoder–Decoder deep dive. That gave us the model — the function that maps a sequence of tokens to a distribution over the next token. It does not, by itself, give us an LLM.

An LLM, in the form people actually use, is that architecture wrapped in a much larger system: a way to turn raw text into tokens at scale, a training loop that can run across many GPUs for weeks, a second training stage that turns a raw next-token predictor into something that follows instructions, and finally an inference engine that can serve that model to many users at once without recomputing work it has already done. This page is the map for that system — the "engine" around the model.

Intuition Think of the Transformer architecture as the engine block of a car — it's the part with all the interesting physics. But an engine block alone doesn't drive anywhere. You still need fuel (data pipelines), an ignition and drivetrain (the training loop), a way to tune it for a specific driver (alignment), and a chassis that can actually carry passengers on a real road at speed (the serving/inference engine). Most of what makes ChatGPT-style systems feel instant and coherent lives in that second set of pieces, not in the attention equation itself.

The Chinchilla Shift: Balancing Parameters and Data

For a long time, the prevailing wisdom in large language model development (led by OpenAI's 2020 GPT-3 scaling laws) was that models should be massively over-parameterized while trained on a relatively fixed budget of tokens. This changed in early 2022 when DeepMind published the Chinchilla scaling laws, completely altering how engineers approach model sizing.

Intuition Imagine you have a fixed computing budget (say, $10,000 worth of GPU hours). Should you build a massive model and train it on a small amount of text, or a smaller model and train it on a vast ocean of text? Before Chinchilla, labs built the massive model. Chinchilla proved they were starving the model of data—both parameters and tokens need to scale at an equal rate.

Mathematically, training loss $L$ as a function of parameters $N$ and tokens $D$ under a fixed compute budget follows a power law:

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$

Through rigorous experimentation, DeepMind found that $\alpha \approx \beta \approx 0.5$. Because the exponents are roughly equal, if you double your compute budget, you should double your parameter count ($N$) and double your token count ($D$) simultaneously.

Metric The Old Assumption (GPT-3 Era) The Chinchilla Optimal Rule
Token-to-Parameter Ratio ~6 tokens per parameter (e.g., 175B model on 300B tokens) ~20 tokens per parameter (e.g., 70B model on 1.4T tokens)
Inference Cost Impact Terrible: Oversized models waste memory bandwidth during serving Optimized: Smaller models trained longer achieve the same accuracy and run much faster

This realization is why modern open-weights models (like Llama or Mistral) routinely train on tens of trillions of tokens for models with only 7B to 70B parameters—they are strictly "Chinchilla-optimal" or even "over-trained" (trained past the optimal token threshold) so they can run faster and cheaper at inference time.

From Base Model to Assistant: SFT and Alignment

When a pretraining loop finishes, you have a Base Model. If you prompt a base model with "What is the capital of France?", it won't answer the question. Instead, because it is a pure next-token predictor, it will autocomplete the prompt into an internet-style quiz: "What is the capital of France? What is the capital of Germany? What is the capital of Spain?".

Turning that raw text-completer into a conversational assistant requires two distinct post-training stages: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) and Alignment.

Intuition Pretraining teaches the model the structure of human language and facts about the world (reading every book in the library). SFT teaches it the format of a conversation (how to act like a helpful responder rather than an autocomplete engine). Alignment teaches it human values and safety (how to be harmless, honest, and helpful instead of toxic or easily jailbroken).

Stage 1: Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT)

SFT takes the base model and fine-tunes its weights using standard cross-entropy loss on a tightly curated dataset of prompt-response pairs written by humans or distilled from larger models. While pretraining relies on raw scale (trillions of web tokens), SFT relies heavily on craftsmanship and quality over sheer volume.

Anatomy of a "Tightly Curated" SFT Dataset

A high-performing SFT dataset is not just a random scrape of chat logs; it is meticulously engineered across four dimensions:

  • Human-Authored Craftsmanship: High-purity prompts written by domain experts, educators, and professional annotators. These responses model ideal tone, multi-step reasoning, markdown formatting, and explicit edge-case handling (e.g., how to politely decline unsafe requests).
  • Teacher-Model Distillation (Synthetic Data): Leveraging frontier models (like GPT-4 or Claude) to generate ideal answers for complex programmatic, math, or creative prompts. The open-source community relies heavily on distilled datasets (like LMSYS Chatbot Arena dumps or OpenAssistant exports) to bridge the data gap.
  • Rigorous Diversity & Coverage: Ensuring the dataset spans a wide domain taxonomy—coding, logical reasoning, creative writing, roleplay, summarization, and instruction-following constraints (e.g., "Write a response in under 50 words without using the letter 'e'").
  • Deduplication and Quality Filtering: Stripping out repetitive phrasing, sycophantic filler (e.g., excessive apologies or robotic intros like "Sure, I can help with that!"), and factual errors to prevent the model from learning bad conversational habits.

During SFT training itself, a crucial technique called Loss Masking is applied:

The Mechanics of SFT Training
  • Data format: User: [Instruction] \n Assistant: [Ideal Response]
  • Masking: Unlike pretraining where the model computes loss on every single token, in SFT the loss is masked on the user prompt tokens. The model's error gradients are only calculated on the Assistant's response, teaching it to focus purely on generating optimal completions rather than memorizing how to rewrite user prompts.

So in loss masking you typically only compute loss on the response tokens, not the prompt tokens. The prompt is context the model conditions on; you don't want to train it to predict the prompt itself. In SFT, forward pass, backward pass, optimizer — identical to pretraining. What changes is what you show the model and which tokens count toward the loss.

A Closer Look: Loss Masking

This is the single detail students trip on most, and usually for the same reason: "masking" is used in three different, unrelated senses in Transformer literature, and it's easy to assume they're all the same mechanism.

Three different "maskings" — don't conflate them
  • Causal (attention) masking — controls which tokens a given position is allowed to look at inside self-attention. In a Decoder-only model this is always lower-triangular: token $t$ can attend to tokens $1 \dots t$, never to the future. This is present in every forward pass — pretraining, SFT, and inference alike. It never changes.
  • Padding masking — a batching implementation detail. When you pack sequences of different lengths into one batch, shorter ones are padded; padding-masking just tells attention (and the loss) to ignore those pad positions. Unrelated to prompts vs. responses.
  • Loss masking — the one that's specific to SFT. It doesn't touch attention at all. It only decides which token positions contribute to the loss you back-propagate. Every token, prompt included, still passes through the full forward computation; masked tokens are simply excluded from the sum that produces the scalar loss.
Students often assume loss-masked prompt tokens are "hidden" from the model the way padding or future tokens are. They aren't. The model still reads every prompt token as context — attention still flows over them normally. Loss masking changes the gradient signal, not what the model can see.
prompt token — masked out of the loss response token — counted in the loss

Every token — grey and green — still takes part in the forward pass and in attention. Loss masking only decides which positions' prediction errors get summed into the training loss.

Worked Example, With Numbers

Tokenize "User: What is 2+2?\nAssistant: 4<eos>" into a single sequence of 7 tokens (simplified — a real BPE tokenizer would split these further):

Position 1234567
Token User:Whatis2+2?Assistant:4<eos>
Loss mask $m_t$ 0000011

The model still computes a next-token prediction at every position (that's how a Transformer forward pass works — it's parallel across positions). But the loss only sums the masked-in positions:

$$\mathcal{L}_{SFT} = -\frac{1}{\sum_t m_t}\sum_{t=1}^{T} m_t \cdot \log P_\theta(x_t \mid x_{

Here that's just two terms: $-\log P(\text{"4"} \mid \text{"User: What is 2+2?}\backslash\text{nAssistant:"})$ and $-\log P(\langle\text{eos}\rangle \mid \dots, \text{"4"})$, averaged over 2 (not 7). Compare this to the plain pretraining loss, which would sum all 7 positions (predicting "What" from "User:", "is" from "User: What", and so on) — i.e. $m_t = 1$ everywhere.

Implementation detail that trips people up: the denominator matters. If you sum the masked losses but divide by the full sequence length $T$ instead of $\sum_t m_t$ (the actual count of response tokens), short responses get artificially small gradients and long responses get artificially large ones — a subtle bug that shows up as inconsistent SFT quality across examples of different response length, not as an error or a crash.

For mathematically mature readers: mathematical definition of SFT

In supervised fine-tuning (SFT) of autoregressive language models, the training sequence consists of a concatenated tokenized prompt $X = (x_1, x_2, \dots, x_m)$ and a target response $Y = (y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n)$, forming a complete sequence $S = (s_1, s_2, \dots, s_{m+n})$. We introduce a binary indicator mask $m_t \in \{0, 1\}$ for each token position $t$, restricting gradient backpropagation exclusively to the response tokens. Formally, the masked cross-entropy loss $\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}$ is defined as:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\frac{1}{\sum_{t=1}^{m+n} m_t} \sum_{t=1}^{m+n} m_t \log P_\theta(s_t \mid s_{

where $m_t = 0$ for all prompt tokens ($t \le m$) and $m_t = 1$ for all response tokens ($t > m$). Operationally, this masking process is implemented through the following steps:

  1. Logit Generation: The model processes the full sequence $S$ through its layers to produce raw output logits for each token position $t$.
  2. Probability Normalization: The softmax function is applied across the vocabulary dimension at every position $t$ to compute the predictive probability distribution $P_\theta(s_t \mid s_{
  3. Pointwise Cross-Entropy: We calculate the log-probability of the ground-truth token at every position, yielding a vector of losses $\ell_t = -\log P_\theta(s_t \mid s_{
  4. Mask Application: We apply the mask $m_t$ element-wise to the loss vector, effectively setting $\ell_t = 0$ for all prompt tokens ($t \le m$). This ensures that prompt tokens contribute null gradients to the model parameters $\theta$, i.e., $\nabla_\theta \ell_t = 0$ for $t \le m$.
  5. Gradient Normalization: The total scalar loss is calculated by averaging only over the valid response tokens ($\sum m_t$). This normalization is critical to ensure that gradient updates maintain a consistent scale regardless of variations in prompt length across different training examples.

Stage 2: Preference Alignment (RLHF vs. DPO)

Even after meticulous SFT, a model can hallucinate, produce toxic language, or write sycophantic answers. Alignment forces the model to prefer high-quality, safe responses over low-quality ones. There are two primary paradigms for this:

Paradigm How It Works Engineering Trade-offs
RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) 1. Train a separate Reward Model to score responses.
2. Use PPO (Proximal Policy Optimization) to update the LLM policy based on reward scores while penalizing drift from the SFT model. 		Highly complex to run stable distributed training; requires managing 3 to 4 massive models simultaneously in GPU memory (Policy, Reference, Value, Reward).
DPO (Direct Preference Optimization) Skips the reward model entirely. It mathematically reformulates the RL objective so you can optimize the LLM directly on pairs of preferred vs. dispreferred text using a standard cross-entropy loss. Much simpler implementation and stable training loop; has largely replaced PPO for open-weights community alignment.

Mathematically, the DPO loss function optimizes the policy $\pi_\theta$ directly using data pairs $(y_w, y_l)$ representing winning (preferred) and losing (dispreferred) responses:

$$\mathcal{L}_{DPO}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_w|x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l|x)}{\pi_{ref}(y_l|x)} \right) \right]$$

Training Engine vs. Inference Engine

It's worth being explicit early on that "training" and "inference" are optimized for almost opposite things, which is why they end up as two separate engineering problems rather than one:

Dimension Training Engine Inference Engine
Primary goal Maximize throughput across a fixed, known dataset Minimize latency per request, for requests it hasn't seen
Batch structure Large, static batches; padding is cheap Dynamic, continuously arriving requests of varying length
Memory pressure Activations + gradients + optimizer state KV cache growing per token, per active request
Precision Mixed precision (fp16/bf16) for stable gradients Aggressively quantized (int8/int4) where quality allows
Typical bottleneck Compute (FLOPs) and inter-GPU communication Memory bandwidth, not compute
Working Definition An LLM engine, for the purposes of this series, is the full pipeline that:
  1. Converts raw text into a fixed vocabulary of tokens (tokenizer).
  2. Trains a Transformer to predict the next token over a large corpus (pretraining loop).
  3. Adapts that base model to follow instructions and match human preferences (SFT + alignment).
  4. Serves the resulting weights efficiently to many concurrent users (inference engine).
Everything we build from here is one of these four stages.
