Introduction

Non-parametric models

Most classifiers you meet first are parametric: you assume the data has a particular shape (say, each class is Gaussian), and then fit a small, fixed number of parameters — a mean and a covariance per class, for instance. Once fitted, the training data itself can be thrown away; only those few numbers are kept, and they're used to answer every future query. The catch is that the whole model lives or dies by that shape assumption. If the real data isn't Gaussian-shaped, a Gaussian classifier will misfit it in a systematic, uncorrectable way, no matter how much data you feed it.

Non-parametric models make no rigid a priori structural or distributional assumptions about the underlying data-generation process, distinguishing them from parametric architectures such as Support Vector Machines or artificial neural networks(ANN). Consequently, they bypass explicit parameter optimization via empirical risk minimization, objective cost functions, and gradient-based optimization algorithms. Instead of fitting a fixed formula, they let the training data itself stand in for the model — predictions are made by directly examining the training points near the query, and the effective complexity can grow as more data arrives. K-NN is a non-parametric method in exactly this sense: nothing is "trained" and thrown away; the entire labelled set is kept, and every prediction re-examines it from scratch. The general formula below is the starting point for this whole family of non parametric methods.

K-NN (and KDE alongside it) is built on a geometric concept: for a small region of volume — the sphere shown below — V around a point, containing K of N total training samples,

the general estimator follows the formula \[ p(x) \approx \frac{K}{NV} \]

where K is the number of points enclosed within the volume, and V is the volume of the sphere.

This single formula has two parameters to control, V and K. Holding either one fixed and solving for the other gives a different named method: fix V and you get KDE; fix K and you get K-NN. K-NN is our point of discussion here, but it's worth briefly studying the other method first: seeing what it fixes, and where that choice runs into trouble, is exactly what motivates why K-NN fixes the other variable instead. Without that contrast, "K-NN fixes K" reads as an arbitrary design decision rather than a deliberate fix.

Kernel density estimation (KDE), briefly: pick a fixed window size V — a bandwidth h — and slide it around the space. At any point, K is simply however many of the N samples happen to land inside that fixed window. Plug that count into p(x) ≈ K/(NV) and you have a density estimate at every point, without ever assuming the density has a particular shape (Gaussian, uniform, etc.).

Kernel density estimation fixes V and reads off whatever K falls inside. But a single fixed window size is a problem: in a dense region it's too coarse and blurs real structure, and in a sparse region it may catch very few or zero points, making the estimate noisy or collapse to zero. This is exactly the shortcoming K-NN density estimation is built to fix: it does the reverse, fixing K and growing a sphere until it contains exactly that many points, whatever volume V that takes — so the window automatically shrinks in dense regions and expands in sparse ones. That's the version we'll build on next, applied once per class within the same sphere, to get an actual classifier.

K-Nearest Neighbour Classification

K-Nearest Neighbours starts from a simple idea: to decide what a new point is, look at the labelled points sitting closest to it and let them vote. Picture the new point as unlabelled — shown in red just because its class isn't known yet. Grow a sphere around it and see which colour surrounds it most: if most of the nearby points are teal, the new point is likely teal; if most are violet, it's likely violet; if most are gold, it's likely gold. Fig. 0 below shows exactly this — a sphere grown around a query point x until it catches its K nearest neighbours, regardless of which class each one belongs to. The rest of this page works out, precisely, how that simple picture turns into an actual estimate of class probability.

KNN: Real Time classification Problem

A clinic has been recording two measurements from every patient it sees — say, a metabolic marker and an inflammation marker — alongside the diagnosis that was eventually confirmed for that patient. Over time this produces a labelled set: N historical patients, each falling into one of three confirmed classes, C₁, C₂, C₃.

Each patient is a point plotted using their two feature values — metabolic marker on one axis, inflammation marker on the other. The classification job is: given those two numbers, decide which of C₁, C₂, or C₃ that patient most likely belongs to.

Here's how the two features actually get used for that decision, tying back to what's already on the page:

  1. A new patient's two measurements place them at a point x in that 2D space.
  2. A sphere is grown around, shown in the figure below x using distance computed from those two features — that's what "closest" means (Euclidean distance between the two coordinate values).
  3. The sphere expands until it catches K historical patients, whichever diagnoses those happen to be.
  4. The new patient is assigned to whichever class is most common among those K neighbours — p(Ck | x) = Kk / K.

So the two features never disappear or get replaced by anything — they're what defines "closeness" at every step. C1, C2, C3 is just the output, not another input.

Probabilistic Aspects of K-NN

Suppose the historical set has N patients in total, with Nk of them confirmed as class Ck, so that Σk Nk = N. To classify the new point x, draw a single sphere centred on x and let it grow — ignoring class labels entirely — until it contains precisely K points in total. Call the resulting volume V.

Now look inside that same sphere and count by class: it contains Kk points belonging to class Ck. Three quantities fall out of this one construction:

Class-conditional

p(x | Ck) = Kk / (Nk V)

Unconditional

p(x) = K / (N V)

Class prior

p(Ck) = Nk / N

Nothing new is being assumed here. The class-conditional density is just the ordinary K-NN density estimate, run only on the Nk patients who belong to class Ck, evaluated using the same volume V that the shared sphere happened to need. The prior is simply the historical class frequency. All three share the one sphere drawn around x.

Bayes' theorem for K-NN

Plug the three quantities above into Bayes' theorem and watch what cancels:

posterior class membership \[ p(C_k \mid x) = \frac{p(x \mid C_k)\, p(C_k)}{p(x)} = \frac{\dfrac{K_k}{N_k V} \cdot \dfrac{N_k}{N}}{\dfrac{K}{NV}} \;=\; \frac{K_k}{K} \]

Every quantity that depended on where the sphere was drawn — N, V, even Nk — cancels out. What survives is embarrassingly simple: the posterior probability of class Ck is just the fraction of the K neighbours that belong to it. The familiar "majority vote among K nearest neighbours" isn't a heuristic bolted onto K-NN — it is Bayes' theorem, once the class-conditional densities are estimated this way.

This is also the cleanest way to see that K-NN classification is Bayes-derived without being "Bayesian" in the modern sense of the word. There's no prior placed over model parameters and no posterior computed over a parameter space — there are no parameters here at all. It's a plug-in, nonparametric estimate of class-conditional densities, fed through Bayes' rule exactly once.

Finally the K-NN Algorithm

  1. # Input: training set D = {(x_i, y_i)}, test point x_q, parameter K
  3. for each (x_i, y_i) in D:
  4. d_i = distance(x_q, x_i) # e.g. Euclidean
  6. sort points by d_i ascending
  7. N_K(x_q) = the K points with smallest d_i
  9. for each class c:
  10. votes[c] = count of points in N_K(x_q) with label c
  12. p(C_c | x_q) = votes[c] / K # the derivation above, applied
  13. y_q = argmax_c votes[c] # predicted label
  15. return y_q, p(C_c | x_q) for all c

Two implementation notes worth flagging to anyone coding this up: features should be standardised before computing distance, since an unscaled feature with a larger numeric range will dominate the distance calculation regardless of its actual relevance; and there is no training phase — the sort over distances happens fresh at every query, which is why K-NN is called lazy learning.

K-NN in modern NLP and LLM systems

K-NN is a "classical" method, but the same nearest-neighbour idea shows up directly inside modern language systems — just with text embeddings standing in for the two clinical markers, and a much larger N.

1. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG)

At its core, the distance-based search engine behind K-NN is the exact same mechanism that powers modern vector retrieval in AI. Text documents and user queries are converted into dense numerical vectors, allowing the system to use Euclidean or cosine distance to find the K nearest neighbor chunks. Structurally, it shares the familiar K-NN idea of growing a neighborhood around a query point until it catches K matches; however, because there is no classification label or voting step, these neighbors aren't tallied into a category. Instead, they are retrieved as raw reference text and fed directly into an LLM's context window to ground its response.

2. Nearest-neighbour language models (kNN-LM)

K-NN can also directly augment a trained language model. At each generation step, alongside the model's own next-token probabilities, the system performs a K-NN lookup over a huge datastore of (context representation → next token) pairs built from the training data, and interpolates the model's distribution with the distribution implied by those retrieved neighbours. Notably, this needs no retraining of the underlying model — a direct illustration of the "lazy learning" property from Section 6: the datastore is just kept and re-examined at inference time, exactly like the training set in ordinary K-NN.
