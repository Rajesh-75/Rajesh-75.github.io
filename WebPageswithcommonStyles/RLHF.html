Reinforcement Learning via Human Feedback(RLHF)

3.1 What is Reinforcement Learning (RL)?

Before diving into Large Language Models and alignment, we must understand the foundational mechanics of Reinforcement Learning (RL). Unlike supervised learning—where a dataset explicitly tells the model the exact "correct" answer for every input—an RL agent learns entirely through trial and error by interacting with an environment.

An Intuitive Analogy: Training a Dog

To build intuition for how RL works, imagine training a dog to sit on command. The dog starts with no prior instruction and figures out what to do based on consequences:

  • State ($s_t$): The dog's current position and posture at time step $t$ (e.g., standing up or wandering around).
  • Action ($a_t$): What the dog chooses to do next (e.g., sit down, bark, or run away).
  • Reward ($r_t$): The feedback signal. Giving the dog a tasty treat yields a positive reward ($+1$), while ignoring or correcting bad behavior yields a neutral or negative reward ($0$ or $-1$).
  • Policy ($\pi$): The dog's internal decision-making strategy. Over time, the policy learns which actions yield treats for specific commands.
  • Transition: How the physical world updates. If the dog is standing and decides to bend its legs, the physical laws of movement transition it into a new posture ($s_{t+1}$).

Figure 4: Standard RL loop — the agent πθ(·) picks an action at given the current state; the environment returns the next state st and a reward rt.

Notice that Figure 4 writes the policy as $\pi_\theta(\cdot)$ rather than just $\pi$. The subscript $\theta$ denotes the policy's parameters — in a neural-network policy, these are simply its weights. Framed this way, "learning" in RL is really a search over $\theta$: the agent repeatedly acts, observes the resulting state and reward, and adjusts $\theta$ so that future actions accumulate more reward. The RL loop in Figure 4 is exactly this search running again and again, one interaction at a time.

3.1.2 Mapping the RL Loop to Math (The Trajectory Distribution)

In formal reinforcement learning, a complete episode or sequence of interactions (called a trajectory $\tau$) is modeled as a joint probability distribution over states and actions. Mathematically, this trajectory probability is expressed as:

$$p_\pi(\tau) = \rho_0(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t) \, p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$$

Every term in this equation breaks down directly into our components:

  • $\rho_0(s_0)$: The probability of starting at an initial state $s_0$ (e.g., the dog standing at the start of training).
  • $\pi(a_t \mid s_t)$: The Policy — the probability of taking action $a_t$ given the current state $s_t$.
  • $p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$: The Transition Dynamics — the probability of landing in a new state $s_{t+1}$ given the current state and action.

We now have a name for what RL is searching for ($\theta$) and a formal description of the loop it runs (the trajectory distribution above). What's still missing is the actual update rule: a concrete procedure for adjusting $\theta$ after each interaction so that expected reward goes up. This, too, is a general reinforcement learning question, with no connection yet to language models — one widely-used answer is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an RL algorithm that nudges $\theta$ in the direction of higher expected reward while "clipping" each update so the policy doesn't change too drastically in a single step. PPO was developed for general RL problems long before it was adopted for RLHF; we'll reuse it as-is when we get to the policy-update step in Section 3.2.

3.2 Framing the LLM Alignment Problem: Why Standard RL Fails and How RLHF Integrates Human Preferences

Applying traditional Reinforcement Learning directly to large language models breaks down because language lacks the hard, objective, programmatic rules found in chess or video games. Without a ground-truth simulator to say whether a generated paragraph is creative, helpful, or safe, a standard RL loop has no intrinsic way to score success. RLHF closes this gap in three moves:

  • A learned Reward Model replaces the environment's reward. It is trained on human preferences (how, in Section 3.3), so we can steer the model with subjective human intent.
  • A reference model plus a KL penalty anchors the policy, so it cannot wander off and game the reward function.
  • A policy-optimization algorithm (PPO here; DPO is an alternative) optimizes the objective using scalar preference judgments rather than exact token matches against a pretrained or SFT model.

RLHF helps reduce toxic output and makes the model more helpful and safer, but it is not a cure for hallucination: if the reward model favours answers that merely sound authoritative, RLHF can even reward confident mistakes (see the Practical Issues section at the end).

TWO VIEWS OF THE SAME PROCESS

You will meet two descriptions of LLM generation in this section — a contextual bandit (3.2.1) and a token-level MDP (3.2.2). They are not competing claims; they answer two different questions:

  • How is the reward delivered? The Reward Model reads the whole response and returns one score. From the reward's point of view, the whole response is a single "pull of the lever" — the bandit view.
  • How is the policy actually optimized? The model writes one token at a time, so to decide which tokens deserve credit for that one score, optimization treats generation as a step-by-step MDP — the MDP view. This is exactly why the Critic, PPO and GAE exist.

Rule of thumb: one score per response, learning per token.

3.2.1 The High-Level View: The Contextual Bandit

From the perspective of evaluating human preference, the system operates as a single-step Contextual Bandit: a fixed prompt is drawn from a dataset, the policy πθ writes one complete response, and the Reward Model reads that entire completion and returns a single scalar score. Because there's no ongoing back-and-forth with an environment, the whole prompt-to-completion exchange is treated as a single-step interaction, as shown in Figure 5, rather than the many-step trajectory in Figure 4.

INTUITION

What's a "bandit problem"? The name comes from a slot machine — an old "one-armed bandit." You pull the lever once, see what you win, and that's it; there's no state carried forward to the next pull. RLHF treats each prompt the same way: the policy generates one full response, the reward model scores it once, and the interaction ends there — no next state, no planning several moves ahead. That's exactly why the state-transition arrows from Figure 4 (st → st+1) don't appear in Figure 5: every generated token in the response is judged together, as part of one single "pull of the lever," not as a sequence of separate environment steps. (This describes how the reward is delivered. How that single score is turned into a learning signal for individual tokens is the subject of 3.2.2.)

Figure 5: The RLHF Pipeline — flowing sequentially from Prompt Source (1) through Policy Generation (2) and Reward Model evaluation (3) down into the PPO Policy Update (4), emphasizing the single-step bandit nature without complex physical state transitions. The reward $r$ produced in step 3 is the only signal that flows back into step 4, where it scales how much the policy's weights move.

Figure 5 lays out this single-step exchange as a left-to-right pipeline: a prompt is drawn from a fixed source, the policy πθ generates one full completion in response, and the reward model scores that completion once, producing a single scalar reward $r$. The objective being optimized is simply the expected reward, $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[r(p,c)]$, and its gradient, $\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c \mid p) \cdot r(p,c)]$, shows exactly how $r$ is used: it scales the direction that makes a completion more or less likely, so a high-reward completion pulls $\theta$ toward it and a low-reward one pushes $\theta$ away. That reward-scaled update is what flows into the PPO update step introduced in Section 3.1.2 — the same general RL algorithm, unchanged — which adjusts the policy's weights before the next batch of prompts is drawn.

Pulling and Pushing Away: How the Objective Function Updates Behavior

The policy gradient mechanism alters generation probabilities through signed reward scaling:

  1. Log-Probability Gradients: The term $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c \mid p)$ defines the exact direction in weight space that increases the likelihood of generating a given completion.
  2. Pulling Towards (Positive Reward): When $r(p,c) > 0$, multiplying the gradient by a positive scalar keeps the vector aligned. Gradient ascent steps in that direction, increasing the token's probability and pulling it into the model's repertoire.
  3. Pushing Away (Negative/Low Reward): When $r(p,c) < 0$, multiplying by a negative scalar inverts the gradient direction. Gradient ascent then steps away from those parameters, suppressing the probability and pushing bad or unwanted behaviors out of the model.

3.2.2 The Low-Level View: The Autoregressive LLM-MDP

While the reward is delivered globally as in the bandit view above, the model actually generates text sequentially, one token at a time. To update the policy effectively without destroying text coherence, we formalize text generation as a discrete-time, finite-horizon MDP defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$:

  • State Space ($\mathcal{S}$): Defined over the vocabulary alphabet $\mathcal{V}$ as $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{V}^*$, representing all valid token sequences. At time step $t$, the state $s_t = [p; c_{
  • Action Space ($\mathcal{A}$): The vocabulary dictionary $\mathcal{V}$. The action $a_t = c_t$ is the next token sampled from the policy distribution $\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)$.
  • Transition Dynamics ($P$): The environment is the context window, which updates deterministically with no environmental physics:
    $$P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } s_{t+1} = [s_t; a_t] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
  • Horizon ($T$): The sequence terminates when the policy samples a special end-of-sequence token (<EOS>) or hits the maximum context length $T$.

Core Concept: Multi-Step Trajectory vs. Contextual Bandit

To understand why LLM alignment requires a specific framing, developers must distinguish between two types of decision-making loops:

  1. Multi-Step Trajectory (Standard RL / MDP): Actions change the state of the environment step-by-step ($s_t \to s_{t+1}$), and rewards can be delivered intermittently or at multiple checkpoints. Errors ripple forward, requiring temporal credit assignment (like Q-learning or TD learning) to trace a mistake back across time.
  2. Contextual Bandit (RLHF Macro-View): The prompt acts as the context, and the model generates a complete response in one macro-interaction. There are no changing environment dynamics or intermediate world states between tokens. A single global reward is delivered at the end ($t = T$), treating the entire prompt-to-response sequence as a single "pull of the lever."
RECONCILING THE TWO VIEWS

The bandit view is why the reward is a single scalar for the whole response and why there are no environment dynamics to simulate. The MDP view is why training still needs a per-token signal: with one score at the very end, we must work out which individual tokens helped or hurt. Section 3.3 uses both — the Reward Model gives one score at $t=T$ (bandit), and the Critic with GAE spreads credit across tokens (MDP).

Aside: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), covered in Section 3.4, uses the reward signal in a fundamentally different way — it skips the policy-gradient loop above entirely and optimizes directly on pairs of preferred/dispreferred completions. We return to that contrast in Section 3.4, once the PPO picture is complete.

3.3 RLHF Training: Bringing It All Together

Large Language Models start out as raw next-token predictors trained on massive web corpora. While they acquire vast general knowledge during pre-training, they lack conversational etiquette, instruction-following skills, and safety guardrails.

Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) is the critical final alignment stage that solves this. Its core goal is to bridge the gap between raw statistical text generation and subjective human intent. By steering the model using human preferences, RLHF tends to suppress toxic outputs, improves instruction-following and helpfulness, and moves a raw language engine toward a safer conversational assistant (it does not, on its own, eliminate hallucinations).

Overview: Goals and the Three-Stage Journey

To understand why RLHF is necessary, we must view it as the final step in a three-stage training pipeline:

  1. Unsupervised Pre-training: Teaches the model how to speak by predicting the next word across the internet.
  2. Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT): Teaches the model how to be a helpful assistant using curated prompt-response pairs. This creates our starting model (the Reference Model).
  3. RLHF (Alignment): Puts guardrails on the assistant to optimize helpfulness, honesty, and harmlessness based on human preference evaluations.

The Cast of Characters: The Four Models

Because RLHF coordinates multiple systems simultaneously, students often experience cognitive overload. Before diving into the mathematics of the training loop, it helps to know the four distinct roles involved in the process:

  • The Policy ($\pi_\theta$) — The Writer: The active model you are training. It reads the prompt and dynamically generates responses token-by-token.
  • The Reference Model ($\pi_{\text{ref}}$) — The Strict Editor: A frozen copy of the SFT model that stands over the writer's shoulder, enforcing a penalty if the policy drifts too far from its original linguistic style.
  • The Reward Model ($R_\psi$) — The Movie Critic: A frozen model trained on human preferences (how it is trained is explained next) that reads the entire finished response at the end and assigns a single quality score.
  • The Critic ($V_\eta$) — The Running Coach: Evaluates text token-by-token as it's being written to predict future rewards and establish a baseline for advantage estimation.

How the Reward Model Is Trained: Learning from Human Comparisons

So far the Reward Model is just a box that turns a (prompt, response) pair into a score. That box has to be built before the RL stage starts, and it is built from human judgments.

Why comparisons instead of scores? Ask ten people to rate an answer from 1 to 10 and you get ten different scales — one person's 7 is another's 9. Ask the same people "which of these two answers is better?" and they agree far more often. So RLHF collects preferences, not ratings.

Collecting the Preference Data
  1. Sample a prompt $p$.
  2. Let the SFT model generate two different completions for it.
  3. A human labeler picks the better one: the chosen completion $c_w$ ("winner") and the rejected completion $c_l$ ("loser").
  4. Repeat many times to build the dataset $\mathcal{D}_{pref} = \{(p,\, c_w,\, c_l)\}$.

Photosynthesis is the biochemical conversion of photon energy into chemical energy via chlorophyll-mediated carbon fixation.

The model. The Reward Model usually starts from the SFT model, with the next-token output layer replaced by a single-number "head". It reads the prompt and the whole response and outputs one scalar, $R_\psi(p,c)$, at the last token. (This is the same quantity written $RM_\psi$ in the figures, and $r_{RM}$ for one particular response.)

The training objective. We want $R_\psi(p,c_w) > R_\psi(p,c_l)$. The Bradley–Terry model turns the score gap into the probability that a human prefers $c_w$:

$$P(c_w \succ c_l \mid p) = \sigma\big(R_\psi(p,c_w) - R_\psi(p,c_l)\big), \qquad \sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

and training minimizes the negative log-likelihood of the human choices:

$$\mathcal{L}(\psi) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(p,\,c_w,\,c_l)\sim\mathcal{D}_{pref}}\Big[\log \sigma\big(R_\psi(p,c_w) - R_\psi(p,c_l)\big)\Big]$$
INTUITION WITH NUMBERS

Suppose the model gives the chosen answer $1.2$ and the rejected one $0.4$. The gap is $+0.8$, so $\sigma(0.8)\approx 0.69$ and the loss is $-\ln 0.69 \approx 0.37$: small, because the model ranked the pair the way the human did. If it had given them the other way round, the gap would be $-0.8$, $\sigma(-0.8)\approx 0.31$, and the loss would be $-\ln 0.31 \approx 1.17$: large. Gradient descent therefore pushes the chosen score up and the rejected score down.

Two consequences to carry forward:

  • Only differences are trained. The loss depends on the score gap, so the zero point and scale of $R_\psi$ are arbitrary — a score of $2.0$ means little on its own, only relative to other responses.
  • It is a proxy. The Reward Model is a learned imitation of human judgment from a limited set of comparisons, so it can be wrong or exploitable. Once training is done it is frozen and used as the reward signal in RLHF — and its imperfections are exactly why reward hacking (3.3.1) and the KL penalty are needed.

Step-by-Step: What Actually Happens in One RLHF Training Step

One RLHF step runs four models forward, but updates only two of them. Here it is broken down plainly, in the same order it happens.

  1. Prompt p: This is an unlabelled text prompt drawn directly from the training dataset. It acts as the initial input to kick off the loop.
  2. Generate and score — forward pass only, nothing learns yet:
    • Policy $\pi_\theta$ (trainable): writes the response $c$, one token at a time, and records how confident it was at each token — $\log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)$.
    • Reference $\pi_{ref}$ (frozen): the "before" copy of the policy. It reads the same response and records its own confidence at each token — not to generate anything, just so we have something fixed to compare the policy against. That comparison becomes the KL penalty, every token. Its input is the prompt plus the exact response tokens the policy just generated.
    • Reward Model $RM_\psi$ (frozen): reads the whole finished response once and gives it a single score, $r_{RM}$. It never looks token by token — only at the final result.
    • Critic $V_\eta$ (trainable): at every token, estimates how much total reward it expects from this point onward — $V(s_t)$. Its input at position $t$ is the prompt plus only the response tokens generated up to that point (a partial sequence), not the finished response.
  3. Turn those scores into a per-token reward:
    • At every token $t$, the reward is mostly just the KL penalty — how far the policy has drifted from the reference at that step. Only at the very last token ($t=T$) does the reward model's score $r_{RM}$ get added in:
      $$r_t = -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(c_t \mid s_t)} \;+\; \begin{cases} r_{RM} & t = T \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$ The KL term is computed at every token; the reward-model score is added only at the last one.
    • GAE then combines this reward sequence $r_t$ with the critic's estimates $V_\eta(s_t)$ to produce two things: the Advantage $A_t$ (was this token better or worse than the critic expected?) and the Value Target $R_t$ (what the critic should have predicted).
  4. Update weights — the only place backprop happens:
    • Policy update: the Advantage $A_t$ drives the PPO loss, $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t) \cdot A_t$. Backprop updates only $\theta$. (The exact clipped form is in 3.3.3.)
    • Critic update: the gap between $V_\eta(s_t)$ and $R_t$ drives a separate loss, $(V_\eta(s_t) - R_t)^2$. Backprop updates only $\eta$ — a completely separate update from the policy's.

$RM_\psi$ and $\pi_{ref}$ are read from constantly, in every single step — but never once updated. They stay exactly as they were for the entire training run.

3.3.1 Reward Hacking and KL Penalty computation

If you train the AI only to get high scores from the Reward Model, it will learn to cheat. It might repeat text or use formatting tricks just to trick the system. This problem is called reward hacking. To stop this and keep the language natural, we save a copy of the original model. This copy is called the reference model ($\pi_{ref}$). The reference model is frozen and never changes. It acts as a baseline to calculate a penalty (the KL divergence penalty) (i.e penalizes the model from drifting too far from its original baseline). For example, the model might discover that repeating words like "excellent, helpful, fantastic" over and over tricks the Reward Model into giving a huge positive score, resulting in total gibberish that gets a high reward. This keeps the AI stable during training, as shown in the formula:

RLHF Objective & KL Penalty

$$\max_\theta \;\; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim \mathcal{D},\; y \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot \vert x)} \left[ R_\psi(x, y) - \beta \cdot D_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(y \vert x) \,||\, \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \vert x)\big) \right]$$

Here the prompts $x$ come from the dataset, but the completions $y$ are sampled from the current policy $\pi_\theta$ (not taken from a dataset). At the token level, the per-token KL divergence penalty approximation is:

$$\text{KL Penalty}_t \approx \log \left( \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t \vert{} s_t)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(a_t \vert{} s_t)} \right)$$
  • Similar Probabilities (Policy vs. SFT):
    When $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \approx 1 \implies \log(1) = 0$. The penalty is zero.
  • Drastic Over-assignment by Policy:
    When $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} \gg 1 \implies \log(\text{large number}) > 0$. Subtracting this positive value reduces the overall net reward $r_t$.
  • Policy Under-assigns Relative to the Reference:
    When $\frac{\pi_\theta}{\pi_{\text{ref}}} < 1 \implies \log(\text{ratio}) < 0$. Subtracting a negative number gives a small bonus on that token. So an individual token's term can be negative; what is guaranteed to be non-negative is the average over many samples, because the true KL divergence is always $\ge 0$.

KL divergence between two distributions $P$ and $Q$ is defined as:

$$\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q) = \sum_{x} P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$$

Note the sum is over every possible outcome $x$, weighted by how likely $P$ thinks that outcome is. It's a property of the two full distributions — you can't get it from looking at just one outcome; you need to sweep over the entire space.

Why that looks impossible here. In our case, $P = \pi_\theta(c \mid p)$ and $Q = \pi_{ref}(c \mid p)$ — distributions over every possible complete response $c$. If the vocabulary has $|\mathcal{V}|$ tokens and a response is $T$ tokens long, there are $|\mathcal{V}|^T$ possible completions. To literally compute the KL divergence by the formula above, you'd have to enumerate all $|\mathcal{V}|^T$ of them, compute each one's probability under both models, and sum. That's astronomically intractable — and during training you only ever have one sampled completion $c$ per prompt, not the full space. So plugging directly into the textbook definition genuinely can't be done here.

GENERAL CONCEPT: THE MONTE CARLO TRICK

Say you want $\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[f(X)] = \sum_x P(x)\,f(x)$ — go through every possible value $x$, weight it by how likely $P$ says it is, and add them all up. That's fine when there are only a few possible values, but impossible or impractical when there are billions, or the values are continuous.

The fix: instead of summing over everything, just draw samples and average:

$$\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[f(X)] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \quad x_i \sim P$$

As $N$ grows, this average converges to the true expectation — that's the whole trick. Even a single sample is already a valid, if noisy, estimate: roll a fair die once, get a 4, and your one-sample estimate of $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ is $16$ — nowhere near the true value of $\frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17$, but if you repeated "roll once and record" many times and averaged those estimates, you'd land back on 15.17. This is exactly why a single sampled LLM response can be treated as a valid estimate of the KL divergence — no repeated rollouts needed per prompt, since training itself repeats this over many prompts and averages it out.

How it's actually done — sampling, not summing. The resolution isn't magic, it's an identity. Notice the KL formula is itself an expectation:

$$\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right]$$

An expectation can be estimated by sampling instead of summing over everything. If you draw one $c \sim \pi_\theta$ and compute $\log \frac{\pi_\theta(c\mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c\mid p)}$ for just that one sample, that single number is an unbiased single-sample estimate of the true KL divergence — not the exact value, but something that averages out to the exact value over many prompts. That's the standard Monte Carlo trick used everywhere in RL and variational inference, not something specific to RLHF.

GENERAL CONCEPT: THE CHAIN RULE OF PROBABILITY

Any joint probability over a sequence of variables can be broken into a product of conditional probabilities, one variable at a time — this is always true, by the definition of conditional probability, not an approximation:

$$P(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$$

Example: draw 3 cards without replacement and ask for P(Ace, then King, then Queen). Instead of reasoning about all three draws at once, the chain rule lets you go one step at a time, each conditioned on what's already been drawn:

$P(\text{Ace}) \cdot P(\text{King} \mid \text{Ace}) \cdot P(\text{Queen} \mid \text{Ace, King}) = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{4}{51} \cdot \frac{4}{50}$

Since $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$, taking the log of both sides turns this product into a sum:

$$\log P(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$$

A language model generating a response one token at a time is doing exactly this — each token depends on everything generated before it — so the log-probability of the whole response is just the sum of the log-probabilities of each token along the way.

What the chain rule adds on top. The Monte Carlo trick gets you a number from one sample — but it's one lump number for the whole response. The chain-rule decomposition is the separate step that lets you split that single number into $T$ pieces, one per token, so it can be handed out as a per-step penalty during generation instead of only being usable once, at the very end. Because language models are autoregressive, the sequence-level probability decomposes by the chain rule:

$$\log \pi_\theta(c \mid p) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)$$

Applying linearity of expectation, the sequence-level KL divergence expands perfectly into an expected sum of token-level log-ratios:

$$\mathbb{E}_{c \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c \mid p)} \right] = \mathbb{E}_{c \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(c_t \mid s_t)} \right]$$

This is exactly what justifies the per-token reward used during training — the same $r_t$ formula from the Step-by-Step walkthrough below, now derived rather than just asserted:

$$r_t = -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(c_t \mid s_t)} \;+\; \begin{cases} r_{RM}(p,c) & \text{if } t = T \\ 0 & \text{if } t < T \end{cases}$$

The Reward Model ($RM_\psi$) does not give scores word by word. Instead, it reads the whole prompt and the finished response together just once. Then, it gives one single score ($r_{RM}$) for the entire response. Because this score is for the whole text, it cannot be split across individual words. Therefore, during step-by-step training, this final score is added only to the very last token ($t = T$). For all the words before the end ($t < T$), the reward is just the penalty for drifting too far from the baseline.

The Critic ($V_\eta$) is a separate model trained alongside the main AI. Its job is to look at each word as it is written and predict what the final score will be. This prediction serves as a baseline. By comparing the actual result to this baseline, the system calculates an “advantage” score. This score tells the system if a specific word choice was better or worse than expected. The Critic has its own separate weights and its own loss function, meaning it operates independently without sharing parameters.

3.3.2 Credit Allocation via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)

BEFORE THE FORMULAS: VALUE, BASELINE AND ADVANTAGE

Value $V(s_t)$ = "how well do I expect this to end, from here?" Think of an exam. After answering half the paper you can already estimate your final mark. That running estimate is the value. Here, $V(s_t)$ is the Critic's guess of the total reward still to come after the tokens written so far.

Why the raw reward is a poor teacher. If we simply said "the response scored well, so make every token in it more likely", the filler words get pulled up along with the genuinely good choices, and the signal is very noisy. We want to credit a token only for what it contributed beyond what we already expected.

Baseline and advantage. So we subtract a baseline, the expectation. The advantage is "what actually happened minus what we expected". For one step this is the TD error $\delta_t = r_t + V(s_{t+1}) - V(s_t)$: the reward we just received, plus how good things now look, minus how good they looked before this token.

  • Positive advantage: this token left us better off than expected → make it more likely.
  • Negative advantage: worse than expected → make it less likely.

Tiny example. Before writing token $t$ the Critic expects $V(s_t)=0.5$. The token carries a small KL penalty, $r_t=-0.07$, and afterwards the Critic is more optimistic, $V(s_{t+1})=0.8$. Then $\delta_t = -0.07 + 0.8 - 0.5 = +0.23$: this token turned out better than expected, so it is reinforced. Subtracting a baseline does not change the average direction of the gradient, but it removes a lot of noise.

Because the Reward Model's score lands only on the final token, a plain policy-gradient update would be extremely high-variance — one number at the very end would have to explain every token that came before it. The Critic $V_\eta$ addresses this by estimating the expected cumulative future reward from any given partial sequence $s_t$:

$$V_\eta(s_t) \approx \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{l=t}^T \gamma^{l-t} r_l \;\middle\vert{}\; s_t \right]$$

(In standard RLHF, the discount factor $\gamma$ is set to $1$ — there's no reason to discount future tokens within a single response.)

From this estimate, we compute a temporal-difference error at each step and use GAE to distribute credit backward across the whole sequence:

$$\delta_t^V = r_t + \gamma V_\eta(s_{t+1}) - V_\eta(s_t)$$
$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}^V$$

The resulting Advantage $\hat{A}_t$ is exactly the "was this token better or worse than expected?" signal referenced earlier — now with the precise recursion behind it, rather than just the informal description.

READING THE FORMULA: WHAT IS λ?

$\delta_t$ looks only one token ahead: low noise, but it leans entirely on the Critic being right. The other extreme is to look all the way to the end of the response: no reliance on the Critic, but very noisy. The parameter $\lambda \in [0,1]$ blends the two:

  • $\lambda = 0$: $\hat{A}_t = \delta_t$ (one-step look-ahead only).
  • $\lambda = 1$: $\hat{A}_t$ = (total reward from $t$ to the end) $- V_\eta(s_t)$ (look all the way).
  • In practice $\lambda$ is set between these (a common value is around $0.95$), trading noise against dependence on the Critic. The sum also reaches back from later tokens: a late reward is shared out, with weight $(\gamma\lambda)^l$, to the earlier tokens that led to it.

Terminal convention: after the last token $t=T$ there is nothing left to earn, so $V_\eta(s_{T+1}) = 0$ in $\delta_T$.

3.3.3 The Exact Optimization Objectives

The Step-by-Step walkthrough above writes the policy update as the plain gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t) \cdot A_t$ for intuition — but that's not actually what makes PPO "clipped." The real objective also compares the current policy's probability against the probability under the policy that generated the sample, $\pi_{old}$, via a ratio $r_t(\theta) = \dfrac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{old}(c_t \mid s_t)}$, and then clips how far that ratio is allowed to move in a single update:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right) \right]$$

The clip is what stops a single very large advantage from causing a single very large, destabilizing weight update. The Critic, meanwhile, is trained independently with a standard mean-squared-error loss against the target return $R_t = \hat{A}_t + V_\eta(s_t)$:

$$L^{\text{MSE}}(\eta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \left( V_\eta(s_t) - R_t \right)^2 \right]$$

  • $\hat{A}_2 = \delta_2 + 0.9\,\hat{A}_3 = 0.30 + 0.513 = 0.813$

  • $R_1 = 0.862 + 1.0 = 1.862$
  • $R_2 = 0.813 + 1.2 = 2.013$
  • $R_3 = 0.57 + 1.5 = 2.07$

Sanity check: $R_3 = 2.07$ is exactly the reward actually received from $t=3$ onward, as it should be at the last step.

Step 4 — Critic loss (updates $\eta$ only)

Squared errors $(V(s_t) - R_t)^2$: $(1.0-1.862)^2 = 0.743$, $(1.2-2.013)^2 = 0.661$, $(1.5-2.07)^2 = 0.325$. The mean is $L^{\text{MSE}} \approx 0.576$. The Critic was too pessimistic at every position, so training pushes its estimates up.

WHAT THE EXAMPLE SHOWS
  • Credit flows backward. The Reward Model's score arrived only at $t=3$, yet token 1 receives the largest advantage ($0.862$) because GAE passes later good news back to the earlier tokens that led to it.
  • The KL term is small but present at every token. It nudges the per-token reward without ever overruling the Reward Model's score (with $\beta = 0.1$).
  • Exactly two models learn. Step 4 updates only the Critic ($\eta$) and Step 5 updates only the Policy ($\theta$). The Reward Model and the Reference model were only read from.

  7. The gradient flow rule (gradients only update the policy and the critic; the reference model and reward model remain completely frozen).

Real-Life Analogy of RLHF

ANALOGY

The RLHF training loop closely mirrors human mentorship and creative critique across three distinct roles: the writer and their natural habits act like the Policy and Reference Model, where an inner voice anchors the writer to prevent them from slipping into overly dramatic corporate robot speech (reward hacking); the Reward Model functions like a judge reading an entire essay from start to finish to assign a final score based on overall impact rather than interrupting with micro-grades; and finally, the Critic and GAE operate like a reflective coach who breaks down the flow of a piece to distribute credit and figure out which exact choice turned the tide.

3.4 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO): Alignment Without the RL Loop

The PPO pipeline of Section 3.3 works, but it is heavy: four models in memory, fresh text sampled during training, and many knobs ($\beta$, $\epsilon$, $\lambda$, $\gamma$, the Critic's learning rate). DPO asks a bold question: can we reach the same goal — a policy that follows human preferences while staying close to the reference — using only the preference pairs we already collected, with no separate Reward Model and no RL loop? The answer turns out to be yes.

THE KEY IDEA

The reward is hiding inside the policy. For the RLHF objective there is a closed-form optimal policy, and it can be turned around to express the reward in terms of that policy and the reference model. Substitute this into the Reward Model's preference loss and the Reward Model disappears: we can train the policy directly on the preference pairs.

Step 1: The optimal policy of the KL-regularized objective

For a single prompt $p$, RLHF maximizes expected reward minus the KL penalty:

$$\max_{\pi}\; \mathbb{E}_{c \sim \pi(\cdot \mid p)}\big[R(p,c)\big] \;-\; \beta\, \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi(\cdot \mid p)\,\Vert\,\pi_{ref}(\cdot \mid p)\big)$$

This has a known solution:

$$\pi^*(c \mid p) = \frac{1}{Z(p)}\,\pi_{ref}(c \mid p)\,\exp\!\Big(\frac{R(p,c)}{\beta}\Big), \qquad Z(p) = \sum_{c} \pi_{ref}(c \mid p)\exp\!\Big(\frac{R(p,c)}{\beta}\Big)$$

In words: start from the reference model and re-weight each completion by $e^{R/\beta}$, so higher-reward completions become more likely. A small $\beta$ re-weights aggressively; a large $\beta$ stays close to $\pi_{ref}$. $Z(p)$ is just the normalizer, and it depends only on the prompt.

WHY THIS IS THE OPTIMUM (SHORT ALGEBRA)

Write the objective as $-\beta\sum_c \pi(c)\Big[\log\frac{\pi(c)}{\pi_{ref}(c)} - \frac{R(c)}{\beta}\Big] = -\beta\sum_c \pi(c)\log\frac{\pi(c)}{\pi_{ref}(c)\,e^{R(c)/\beta}}$. Since $\pi_{ref}(c)\,e^{R(c)/\beta} = Z\,\pi^*(c)$, this equals

$$-\beta\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi \,\Vert\, \pi^*) + \beta \log Z(p).$$

The second term does not depend on $\pi$, and KL is never negative, so the objective is largest when the KL term is zero, i.e. when $\pi = \pi^*$.

Step 2: Turn it around — reward in terms of the policy

Take the logarithm of $\pi^*$ and solve for the reward:

$$R(p,c) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*(c \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c \mid p)} + \beta \log Z(p)$$

The reward is the log-ratio of the optimal policy to the reference (scaled by $\beta$), plus a term that depends only on the prompt. $Z(p)$ sums over every possible completion and is intractable — but watch what happens next.

Step 3: Plug into the Bradley–Terry preference loss

Section 3.3 trained the Reward Model with $-\log\sigma\big(R(p,c_w) - R(p,c_l)\big)$. That depends only on the difference of two rewards for the same prompt, so the $\beta\log Z(p)$ terms cancel:

$$R(p,c_w) - R(p,c_l) = \beta \log\frac{\pi^*(c_w \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c_w \mid p)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi^*(c_l \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c_l \mid p)}$$

Replace the unknown $\pi^*$ with the policy we are training, $\pi_\theta$. This gives the DPO loss:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(p,\,c_w,\,c_l)\sim\mathcal{D}_{pref}}\left[\log \sigma\!\left(\beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(c_w \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c_w \mid p)} - \beta \log\frac{\pi_\theta(c_l \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c_l \mid p)}\right)\right]$$

Reading the DPO loss

  • Implicit reward. The quantity $\hat{r}_\theta(p,c) = \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(c \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c \mid p)}$ plays the role of the Reward Model's score. The policy is its own reward model.
  • What training does. It raises the chosen completion's probability relative to the reference and lowers the rejected one's. Both log-probabilities are sums of token log-probabilities (the chain rule from 3.3.1).
  • The KL anchor is built in. $\pi_{ref}$ appears inside the loss, so the policy is measured against the reference throughout, with no separate penalty term. As in PPO, a larger $\beta$ keeps the policy closer to the reference.
  • Hard pairs get bigger updates. The gradient is scaled by $\sigma(-\text{margin})$, where the margin is the quantity inside the $\sigma$ above. A pair the model currently ranks the wrong way has a negative margin, so it gets a large weight; a pair it already ranks correctly gets a small one.
WORKED NUMBERS

Take $\beta = 0.1$. For the chosen completion, $\log\pi_\theta(c_w \mid p) = -10.0$ and $\log\pi_{ref}(c_w \mid p) = -11.0$, so its log-ratio is $+1.0$. For the rejected completion, $\log\pi_\theta(c_l \mid p) = -15.0$ and $\log\pi_{ref}(c_l \mid p) = -14.0$, so its log-ratio is $-1.0$.

The margin is $0.1\times\big(1.0 - (-1.0)\big) = 0.2$, so $\sigma(0.2) \approx 0.55$ and the loss is $-\ln 0.55 \approx 0.60$. At the very start of training $\pi_\theta = \pi_{ref}$, so the margin is $0$, $\sigma(0)=0.5$ and the loss is $\ln 2 \approx 0.693$. Training drives the loss down by widening the gap between the chosen and rejected log-ratios.

Takeaway

DPO is not a different goal; it is the same KL-regularized objective, solved by algebra instead of by running an RL loop. This is the first of the two industry mitigations named in the Practical Issues section below. Both approaches are used in practice: DPO and its variants are popular because they are simple, while online RL methods such as PPO remain in use when fresh on-policy sampling or flexible reward signals matter.

Practical Issues of Training LLM and RLHF

While post-training and RLHF are highly effective in theory, implementing them in production reveals severe bottlenecks. Training Large Language Models (LLMs) and aligning them with human preferences involves managing complex data quality issues, engineering stability bugs, and wrestling with computational resource constraints.

The primary practical issues encountered when training LLMs and executing RLHF pipelines break down into three major categories.

1. Data and Human Feedback Bottlenecks

In post-training, data quality is the ultimate bottleneck; no amount of architectural sophistication or optimization gymnastics can compensate for noisy, misaligned, or poorly curated human preference data, making robust data engineering and alignment pipeline governance the single hardest engineering challenge in the stack.

  • The Length Bias Exploitation: In LLM optimization loop is successfully doing exactly what you programmed it to do (maximizing the reward score), while completely failing at what you actually want it to do (writing good answers). Fixing it requires constant architectural mitigation, dataset re-balancing, and meticulous hyperparameter babysitting.
  • Human Evaluator Inconsistency & Fatigue: Human annotators are not a monolith. They carry diverse cultural biases, disagree on subjective tasks, and suffer from attention decay over long sessions. Data poisoning (deliberately bad annotations) is also a constant threat.
  • The "Syndicated Plausibility" Trap: Humans struggle to accurately evaluate tasks where the AI exhibits specialized knowledge (like auditing complex code or advanced mathematics). Annotators routinely reward responses that look authoritative and correct, unintentionally training the model to become a highly convincing hallucinator.

2. Algorithmic and Training Instability

Reinforcement learning is notoriously volatile, and applying it to models with billions of parameters introduces distinct failure modes.

  • Reward Hacking: The active LLM quickly figures out how to game the system. It discovers loopholes or specific formatting patterns that maximize the score from the Reward Model without actually satisfying the true human intent.
  • Policy Collapse and Degeneration: If the RL training steps are too aggressive, the LLM suffers from "catastrophic forgetting." It can lose its baseline linguistic fluency, starting to repeat specific keywords endlessly or refusing to answer benign queries due to over-tightened safety constraints.
  • Reference Drift: To prevent policy collapse, developers use a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to keep the active model close to the original fine-tuned model. Balancing this penalty is a massive headache: too high, and the model doesn't learn human preferences; too low, and the model's text generation breaks down completely.

3. Computational and Hardware Challenges

The infrastructure required to execute full-scale RLHF is an engineering nightmare that frequently triggers system crashes.

Issue Cause Practical Impact
VRAM Volatility PPO pipelines require hosting up to four models simultaneously (Policy, Reference, Reward, and Value networks). Inevitable "CUDA out of memory" errors on NVIDIA graphics cards. It demands massive clusters of linked GPUs to run even a single training loop.
High Sample Inefficiency RL requires sampling millions of different text outputs by trial and error to learn a stable pattern. High cloud computing costs and lengthy iteration cycles, making the process highly expensive.
Distributed Orchestration Splitting massive models across hundreds of physical GPUs requires highly complex tensor and pipeline parallelism. A single networking hiccup or hardware fault can desynchronize gradients, corrupting the entire training run mid-way.

How the Industry Mediates These Challenges

To bypass these issues, modern engineering teams heavily rely on two shifts:

  1. Transitioning to DPO: Moving from PPO to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) eliminates the separate reward and value models entirely. This cuts infrastructure requirements in half and bypasses the instability of training a separate reinforcement learning agent.
  2. Adopting RLAIF: Replacing human annotators with AI Feedback (RLAIF) uses frontier models (like GPT-4 or Claude) as judges. AI judges operate instantly, do not suffer from fatigue, can handle complex logical tasks, and scale at a fraction of the cost.
