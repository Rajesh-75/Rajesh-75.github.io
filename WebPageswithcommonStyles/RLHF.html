3.1 What is Reinforcement Learning (RL)?

Before diving into Large Language Models and alignment, we must understand the foundational mechanics of Reinforcement Learning (RL). Unlike supervised learning—where a dataset explicitly tells the model the exact "correct" answer for every input—an RL agent learns entirely through trial and error by interacting with an environment.

An Intuitive Analogy: Training a Dog

To build intuition for how RL works, imagine training a dog to sit on command. The dog starts with no prior instruction and figures out what to do based on consequences:

  • State ($s_t$): The dog's current position and posture at time step $t$ (e.g., standing up or wandering around).
  • Action ($a_t$): What the dog chooses to do next (e.g., sit down, bark, or run away).
  • Reward ($r_t$): The feedback signal. Giving the dog a tasty treat yields a positive reward ($+1$), while ignoring or correcting bad behavior yields a neutral or negative reward ($0$ or $-1$).
  • Policy ($\pi$): The dog's internal decision-making strategy. Over time, the policy learns which actions yield treats for specific commands.
  • Transition: How the physical world updates. If the dog is standing and decides to bend its legs, the physical laws of movement transition it into a new posture ($s_{t+1}$).

Notice that Figure 4 writes the policy as $\pi_\theta(\cdot)$ rather than just $\pi$. The subscript $\theta$ denotes the policy's parameters — in a neural-network policy, these are simply its weights. Framed this way, "learning" in RL is really a search over $\theta$: the agent repeatedly acts, observes the resulting state and reward, and adjusts $\theta$ so that future actions accumulate more reward. The RL loop in Figure 4 is exactly this search running again and again, one interaction at a time.

3.1.2 Mapping the RL Loop to Math (The Trajectory Distribution)

In formal reinforcement learning, a complete episode or sequence of interactions (called a trajectory $\tau$) is modeled as a joint probability distribution over states and actions. Mathematically, this trajectory probability is expressed as:

$$p_\pi(\tau) = \rho_0(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t) \, p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$$

Every term in this equation breaks down directly into our components:

  • $\rho_0(s_0)$: The probability of starting at an initial state $s_0$ (e.g., the dog standing at the start of training).
  • $\pi(a_t \mid s_t)$: The Policy — the probability of taking action $a_t$ given the current state $s_t$.
  • $p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$: The Transition Dynamics — the probability of landing in a new state $s_{t+1}$ given the current state and action.

We now have a name for what RL is searching for ($\theta$) and a formal description of the loop it runs (the trajectory distribution above). What's still missing is the actual update rule: a concrete procedure for adjusting $\theta$ after each interaction so that expected reward goes up. This, too, is a general reinforcement learning question, with no connection yet to language models — one widely-used answer is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an RL algorithm that nudges $\theta$ in the direction of higher expected reward while "clipping" each update so the policy doesn't change too drastically in a single step. PPO was developed for general RL problems long before it was adopted for RLHF; we'll reuse it as-is when we get to the policy-update step in Section 3.2.

3.2 Why RL cannot be used in LLM, Instead RLHF

Applying classical Reinforcement Learning directly to Large Language Models fails because standard RL assumes an open-ended environment with fixed physical rewards and dynamic state transitions driven by environmental physics. In contrast, language generation requires a specialized formulation where the "state" is a fixed prompt combined with generated tokens, and the reward cannot be measured by a static rulebook. Instead, Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) replaces environmental rewards with a learned neural network proxy (the Reward Model) that evaluates an entire response completion at once. Because there's no ongoing back-and-forth with an environment, the whole prompt-to-completion exchange is treated as a single-step interaction, as shown in the figure 5, rather than the many-step trajectory in Figure 4.

INTUITION

What's a "bandit problem"? The name comes from a slot machine — an old "one-armed bandit." You pull the lever once, see what you win, and that's it; there's no state carried forward to the next pull. RLHF treats each prompt the same way: the policy generates one full response, the reward model scores it once, and the interaction ends there — no next state, no planning several moves ahead. That's exactly why the state-transition arrows from Figure 4 (st → st+1) don't appear in Figure 5: every generated token in the response is judged together, as part of one single "pull of the lever," not as a sequence of separate environment steps.

Figure 5: The RLHF Pipeline — flowing sequentially from Prompt Source (1) through Policy Generation (2) and Reward Model evaluation (3) down into the PPO Policy Update (4), emphasizing the single-step bandit nature without complex physical state transitions. The reward $r$ produced in step 3 is the only signal that flows back into step 4, where it scales how much the policy's weights move.

Figure 5 lays out this single-step exchange as a left-to-right pipeline: a prompt is drawn from a fixed source, the policy πθ generates one full completion in response, and the reward model scores that completion once, producing a single scalar reward $r$. The objective being optimized is simply the expected reward, $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[r(p,c)]$, and its gradient, $\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c \mid p) \cdot r(p,c)]$, shows exactly how $r$ is used: it scales the direction that makes a completion more or less likely, so a high-reward completion pulls $\theta$ toward it and a low-reward one pushes $\theta$ away. That reward-scaled update is what flows into the PPO update step introduced in Section 3.1.2 — the same general RL algorithm, unchanged — which adjusts the policy's weights before the next batch of prompts is drawn.

Aside: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), covered in a later section, uses the reward signal in a fundamentally different way — it skips the policy-gradient loop above entirely and optimizes directly on pairs of preferred/dispreferred completions. We'll return to that contrast once the PPO picture is complete.

3.3 RLHF Training

During RLHF training, optimizing a policy model solely to maximize the Reward Model's score can cause the model to exploit loopholes, generate repetitive text, or use superficial formatting tricks to artificially inflate its reward — a manifestation of Goodhart's Law known as reward hacking. To prevent this severe drift and preserve core language fluency, a copy of the Supervised Fine-Tuned model is saved as a reference model ($\pi_{ref}$). Because this reference model is frozen, its weights are static and do not receive gradient updates; its sole function is to serve as a baseline probability distribution for computing the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence penalty against the active policy. This penalizes the policy whenever it strays too far from its original behavior, ensuring a stable balance captured in the PPO objective:

$$\max_\theta \;\; \mathbb{E}\big[RM_\psi(p, c)\big] \;-\; \beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert{}\, \pi_{ref}\big)$$

Unlike traditional reinforcement learning where rewards are distributed continuously across every step, the Reward Model ($RM_\psi$) evaluates the complete prompt and generated response globally, reading the entire sequence just once to output a single scalar score ($r_{RM}$). Because this evaluation applies to the whole response rather than individual words, it cannot be neatly divided across every token position. Consequently, during the token-by-token PPO optimization loop, the Reward Model's score is injected exclusively at the very last token ($t = T$). For all preceding steps ($t < T$), the per-token reward consists solely of the KL divergence penalty.

The Critic ($V_\eta$) is a separate neural network trained alongside the policy model during PPO optimization to serve as a vital value-estimator. Its primary job at each token position is to evaluate the current state and predict the expected total future reward remaining until the end of the response. This prediction acts as a crucial baseline, allowing the algorithm to calculate the “advantage” of individual token choices by determining whether the generation performed better or worse than expected. Operating technically as a regression task, the Critic maintains independent weights and its own separate loss function, enabling it to track and estimate future rewards accurately without sharing parameters with the policy or reward models.

Step-by-Step: What Actually Happens in One RLHF Training Step

One RLHF step runs four models forward, but updates only two of them. Here it is broken down plainly, in the same order it happens.

  1. Generate and score — forward pass only, nothing learns yet:
    • Policy $\pi_\theta$ (trainable): writes the response $c$, one token at a time, and records how confident it was at each token — $\log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)$.
    • Reference $\pi_{ref}$ (frozen): the "before" copy of the policy. It reads the same response and records its own confidence at each token — not to generate anything, just so we have something fixed to compare the policy against. That comparison becomes the KL penalty, every token.
    • Reward Model $RM_\psi$ (frozen): reads the whole finished response once and gives it a single score, $r_{RM}$. It never looks token by token — only at the final result.
    • Critic $V_\eta$ (trainable): at every token, estimates how much total reward it expects from this point onward — $V(s_t)$.
  2. Turn those scores into a per-token reward:
    • At every token $t$, the reward is mostly just the KL penalty — how far the policy has drifted from the reference at that step. Only at the very last token ($t=T$) does the reward model's score $r_{RM}$ get added in:
      $$r_t = -\beta \cdot \text{KL}(\pi_\theta \parallel \pi_{ref})_t \;+\; \begin{cases} r_{RM} & t = T \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
    • GAE then combines this reward sequence $r_t$ with the critic's estimates $V_\eta(s_t)$ to produce two things: the Advantage $A_t$ (was this token better or worse than the critic expected?) and the Value Target $R_t$ (what the critic should have predicted).
  3. Update weights — the only place backprop happens:
    • Policy update: the Advantage $A_t$ drives the PPO loss, $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t) \cdot A_t$. Backprop updates only $\theta$.
    • Critic update: the gap between $V_\eta(s_t)$ and $R_t$ drives a separate loss, $(V_\eta(s_t) - R_t)^2$. Backprop updates only $\eta$ — a completely separate update from the policy's.

$RM_\psi$ and $\pi_{ref}$ are read from constantly, in every single step — but never once updated. They stay exactly as they were for the entire training run.

Summary: The RLHF Training Loop

  1. The Policy's role in generating the text and tracking its own token log-probabilities.
  2. The Reference Model's role as a frozen baseline computing the per-step KL divergence penalty to keep the model anchored.
  3. The Reward Model's role as a global evaluator that scores the entire response at once, with its scalar dropped exclusively at the final token ($t = T$).
  4. The Critic's role in estimating expected future rewards at each step to create a moving baseline.
  5. The GAE calculation merging these signals into advantages ($A_t$) and value targets ($R_t$).
  6. The dual losses (one for the policy, one for the critic).
  7. The gradient flow rule (gradients only update the policy and the critic; the reference model and reward model remain completely frozen).

Real-Life Analogy of RLHF

ANALOGY

The RLHF training loop closely mirrors human mentorship and creative critique across three distinct roles: the writer and their natural habits act like the Policy and Reference Model, where an inner voice anchors the writer to prevent them from slipping into overly dramatic corporate robot speech (reward hacking); the Reward Model functions like a judge reading an entire essay from start to finish to assign a final score based on overall impact rather than interrupting with micro-grades; and finally, the Critic and GAE operate like a reflective coach who breaks down the flow of a piece to distribute credit and figure out which exact choice turned the tide.
