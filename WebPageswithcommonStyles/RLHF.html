3.1 What is Reinforcement Learning (RL)?

Before diving into Large Language Models and alignment, we must understand the foundational mechanics of Reinforcement Learning (RL). Unlike supervised learning—where a dataset explicitly tells the model the exact "correct" answer for every input—an RL agent learns entirely through trial and error by interacting with an environment.

An Intuitive Analogy: Training a Dog

To build intuition for how RL works, imagine training a dog to sit on command. The dog starts with no prior instruction and figures out what to do based on consequences:

  • State ($s_t$): The dog's current position and posture at time step $t$ (e.g., standing up or wandering around).
  • Action ($a_t$): What the dog chooses to do next (e.g., sit down, bark, or run away).
  • Reward ($r_t$): The feedback signal. Giving the dog a tasty treat yields a positive reward ($+1$), while ignoring or correcting bad behavior yields a neutral or negative reward ($0$ or $-1$).
  • Policy ($\pi$): The dog's internal decision-making strategy. Over time, the policy learns which actions yield treats for specific commands.
  • Transition: How the physical world updates. If the dog is standing and decides to bend its legs, the physical laws of movement transition it into a new posture ($s_{t+1}$).

Figure 4: Standard RL loop — the agent πθ(·) picks an action at given the current state; the environment returns the next state st and a reward rt.

Notice that Figure 4 writes the policy as $\pi_\theta(\cdot)$ rather than just $\pi$. The subscript $\theta$ denotes the policy's parameters — in a neural-network policy, these are simply its weights. Framed this way, "learning" in RL is really a search over $\theta$: the agent repeatedly acts, observes the resulting state and reward, and adjusts $\theta$ so that future actions accumulate more reward. The RL loop in Figure 4 is exactly this search running again and again, one interaction at a time.

3.1.2 Mapping the RL Loop to Math (The Trajectory Distribution)

In formal reinforcement learning, a complete episode or sequence of interactions (called a trajectory $\tau$) is modeled as a joint probability distribution over states and actions. Mathematically, this trajectory probability is expressed as:

$$p_\pi(\tau) = \rho_0(s_0) \prod_{t=0}^{T-1} \pi(a_t \mid s_t) \, p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$$

Every term in this equation breaks down directly into our components:

  • $\rho_0(s_0)$: The probability of starting at an initial state $s_0$ (e.g., the dog standing at the start of training).
  • $\pi(a_t \mid s_t)$: The Policy — the probability of taking action $a_t$ given the current state $s_t$.
  • $p(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$: The Transition Dynamics — the probability of landing in a new state $s_{t+1}$ given the current state and action.

We now have a name for what RL is searching for ($\theta$) and a formal description of the loop it runs (the trajectory distribution above). What's still missing is the actual update rule: a concrete procedure for adjusting $\theta$ after each interaction so that expected reward goes up. This, too, is a general reinforcement learning question, with no connection yet to language models — one widely-used answer is Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO), an RL algorithm that nudges $\theta$ in the direction of higher expected reward while "clipping" each update so the policy doesn't change too drastically in a single step. PPO was developed for general RL problems long before it was adopted for RLHF; we'll reuse it as-is when we get to the policy-update step in Section 3.2.

3.2 Framing the LLM Alignment Problem: Why Standard RL Fails and How RLHF Integrates Human Preferences

Applying traditional Reinforcement Learning directly to large language models breaks down because language lacks the hard, objective, programmatic environment rules found in chess or video games. Without a rigid ground-truth simulator to evaluate whether a generated paragraph is creative, helpful, or safe, standard RL loops have no intrinsic way to score success. More critically, text generation is fundamentally a single-step contextual bandit problem rather than a true multi-step trajectory where environment dynamics change based on intermediate actions. To bridge this gap, RLHF replaces traditional environment rewards with a learned Reward Model trained on human preferences. This setup allows us to steer the model using subjective human intent, while a reference model and KL penalty anchor the policy to prevent it from gaming the reward function.

3.2.1 The High-Level View: The Contextual Bandit

From the perspective of evaluating human preference, the system operates as a single-step Contextual Bandit: a fixed prompt is drawn from a dataset, the policy πθ writes one complete response, and the Reward Model reads that entire completion and returns a single scalar score. Because there's no ongoing back-and-forth with an environment, the whole prompt-to-completion exchange is treated as a single-step interaction, as shown in Figure 5, rather than the many-step trajectory in Figure 4.

INTUITION

What's a "bandit problem"? The name comes from a slot machine — an old "one-armed bandit." You pull the lever once, see what you win, and that's it; there's no state carried forward to the next pull. RLHF treats each prompt the same way: the policy generates one full response, the reward model scores it once, and the interaction ends there — no next state, no planning several moves ahead. That's exactly why the state-transition arrows from Figure 4 (st → st+1) don't appear in Figure 5: every generated token in the response is judged together, as part of one single "pull of the lever," not as a sequence of separate environment steps.

Figure 5: The RLHF Pipeline — flowing sequentially from Prompt Source (1) through Policy Generation (2) and Reward Model evaluation (3) down into the PPO Policy Update (4), emphasizing the single-step bandit nature without complex physical state transitions. The reward $r$ produced in step 3 is the only signal that flows back into step 4, where it scales how much the policy's weights move.

Figure 5 lays out this single-step exchange as a left-to-right pipeline: a prompt is drawn from a fixed source, the policy πθ generates one full completion in response, and the reward model scores that completion once, producing a single scalar reward $r$. The objective being optimized is simply the expected reward, $J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[r(p,c)]$, and its gradient, $\nabla_\theta J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}[\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c \mid p) \cdot r(p,c)]$, shows exactly how $r$ is used: it scales the direction that makes a completion more or less likely, so a high-reward completion pulls $\theta$ toward it and a low-reward one pushes $\theta$ away. That reward-scaled update is what flows into the PPO update step introduced in Section 3.1.2 — the same general RL algorithm, unchanged — which adjusts the policy's weights before the next batch of prompts is drawn.

Pulling and Pushing Away: How the Objective Function Updates Behavior

The policy gradient mechanism alters generation probabilities through signed reward scaling:

  1. Log-Probability Gradients: The term $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c \mid p)$ defines the exact direction in weight space that increases the likelihood of generating a given completion.
  2. Pulling Towards (Positive Reward): When $r(p,c) > 0$, multiplying the gradient by a positive scalar keeps the vector aligned. Gradient ascent steps in that direction, increasing the token's probability and pulling it into the model's repertoire.
  3. Pushing Away (Negative/Low Reward): When $r(p,c) < 0$, multiplying by a negative scalar inverts the gradient direction. Gradient ascent then steps away from those parameters, suppressing the probability and pushing bad or unwanted behaviors out of the model.

3.2.2 The Low-Level View: The Autoregressive LLM-MDP

While the reward is delivered globally as in the bandit view above, the model actually generates text sequentially, one token at a time. To update the policy effectively without destroying text coherence, we formalize text generation as a discrete-time, finite-horizon MDP defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, P, r, \gamma)$:

  • State Space ($\mathcal{S}$): Defined over the vocabulary alphabet $\mathcal{V}$ as $\mathcal{S} = \mathcal{V}^*$, representing all valid token sequences. At time step $t$, the state $s_t = [p; c_{
  • Action Space ($\mathcal{A}$): The vocabulary dictionary $\mathcal{V}$. The action $a_t = c_t$ is the next token sampled from the policy distribution $\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)$.
  • Transition Dynamics ($P$): The environment is the context window, which updates deterministically with no environmental physics:
    $$P(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } s_{t+1} = [s_t; a_t] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
  • Horizon ($T$): The sequence terminates when the policy samples a special end-of-sequence token (<EOS>) or hits the maximum context length $T$.

Core Concept: Multi-Step Trajectory vs. Contextual Bandit

To understand why LLM alignment requires a specific framing, developers must distinguish between two types of decision-making loops:

  1. Multi-Step Trajectory (Standard RL / MDP): Actions change the state of the environment step-by-step ($s_t \to s_{t+1}$), and rewards can be delivered intermittently or at multiple checkpoints. Errors ripple forward, requiring temporal credit assignment (like Q-learning or TD learning) to trace a mistake back across time.
  2. Contextual Bandit (RLHF Macro-View): The prompt acts as the context, and the model generates a complete response in one macro-interaction. There are no changing environment dynamics or intermediate world states between tokens. A single global reward is delivered at the end ($t = T$), treating the entire prompt-to-response sequence as a single "pull of the lever."

Aside: Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), covered in a later section, uses the reward signal in a fundamentally different way — it skips the policy-gradient loop above entirely and optimizes directly on pairs of preferred/dispreferred completions. We'll return to that contrast once the PPO picture is complete.

3.3 RLHF Training

If you train the AI only to get high scores from the Reward Model, it will learn to cheat. It might repeat text or use formatting tricks just to trick the system. This problem is called reward hacking. To stop this and keep the language natural, we save a copy of the original model. This copy is called the reference model ($\pi_{ref}$). The reference model is frozen and never changes. It acts as a baseline to calculate a penalty (the KL divergence penalty) whenever the active AI changes too much. This keeps the AI stable during training, as shown in the formula:

$$\max_\theta \;\; \mathbb{E}\big[RM_\psi(p, c)\big] \;-\; \beta \cdot \mathrm{KL}\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert{}\, \pi_{ref}\big)$$

3.3.1 Why the KL Penalty Can Be Computed Per Token

KL divergence between two distributions $P$ and $Q$ is defined as:

$$\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q) = \sum_{x} P(x) \log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}$$

Note the sum is over every possible outcome $x$, weighted by how likely $P$ thinks that outcome is. It's a property of the two full distributions — you can't get it from looking at just one outcome; you need to sweep over the entire space.

Why that looks impossible here. In our case, $P = \pi_\theta(c \mid p)$ and $Q = \pi_{ref}(c \mid p)$ — distributions over every possible complete response $c$. If the vocabulary has $|\mathcal{V}|$ tokens and a response is $T$ tokens long, there are $|\mathcal{V}|^T$ possible completions. To literally compute the KL divergence by the formula above, you'd have to enumerate all $|\mathcal{V}|^T$ of them, compute each one's probability under both models, and sum. That's astronomically intractable — and during training you only ever have one sampled completion $c$ per prompt, not the full space. So plugging directly into the textbook definition genuinely can't be done here.

GENERAL CONCEPT: THE MONTE CARLO TRICK

Say you want $\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[f(X)] = \sum_x P(x)\,f(x)$ — go through every possible value $x$, weight it by how likely $P$ says it is, and add them all up. That's fine when there are only a few possible values, but impossible or impractical when there are billions, or the values are continuous.

The fix: instead of summing over everything, just draw samples and average:

$$\mathbb{E}_{X\sim P}[f(X)] \approx \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} f(x_i), \quad x_i \sim P$$

As $N$ grows, this average converges to the true expectation — that's the whole trick. Even a single sample is already a valid, if noisy, estimate: roll a fair die once, get a 4, and your one-sample estimate of $\mathbb{E}[X^2]$ is $16$ — nowhere near the true value of $\frac{91}{6} \approx 15.17$, but if you repeated "roll once and record" many times and averaged those estimates, you'd land back on 15.17. This is exactly why a single sampled LLM response can be treated as a valid estimate of the KL divergence — no repeated rollouts needed per prompt, since training itself repeats this over many prompts and averages it out.

How it's actually done — sampling, not summing. The resolution isn't magic, it's an identity. Notice the KL formula is itself an expectation:

$$\mathrm{KL}(P \Vert Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x \sim P}\left[\log \frac{P(x)}{Q(x)}\right]$$

An expectation can be estimated by sampling instead of summing over everything. If you draw one $c \sim \pi_\theta$ and compute $\log \frac{\pi_\theta(c\mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c\mid p)}$ for just that one sample, that single number is an unbiased single-sample estimate of the true KL divergence — not the exact value, but something that averages out to the exact value over many prompts. That's the standard Monte Carlo trick used everywhere in RL and variational inference, not something specific to RLHF.

GENERAL CONCEPT: THE CHAIN RULE OF PROBABILITY

Any joint probability over a sequence of variables can be broken into a product of conditional probabilities, one variable at a time — this is always true, by the definition of conditional probability, not an approximation:

$$P(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \prod_{t=1}^{T} P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$$

Example: draw 3 cards without replacement and ask for P(Ace, then King, then Queen). Instead of reasoning about all three draws at once, the chain rule lets you go one step at a time, each conditioned on what's already been drawn:

$P(\text{Ace}) \cdot P(\text{King} \mid \text{Ace}) \cdot P(\text{Queen} \mid \text{Ace, King}) = \frac{4}{52} \cdot \frac{4}{51} \cdot \frac{4}{50}$

Since $\log(ab) = \log a + \log b$, taking the log of both sides turns this product into a sum:

$$\log P(x_1, \ldots, x_T) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log P(x_t \mid x_1, \ldots, x_{t-1})$$

A language model generating a response one token at a time is doing exactly this — each token depends on everything generated before it — so the log-probability of the whole response is just the sum of the log-probabilities of each token along the way.

What the chain rule adds on top. The Monte Carlo trick gets you a number from one sample — but it's one lump number for the whole response. The chain-rule decomposition is the separate step that lets you split that single number into $T$ pieces, one per token, so it can be handed out as a per-step penalty during generation instead of only being usable once, at the very end. Because language models are autoregressive, the sequence-level probability decomposes by the chain rule:

$$\log \pi_\theta(c \mid p) = \sum_{t=1}^T \log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)$$

Applying linearity of expectation, the sequence-level KL divergence expands perfectly into an expected sum of token-level log-ratios:

$$\mathbb{E}_{c \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c \mid p)}{\pi_{ref}(c \mid p)} \right] = \mathbb{E}_{c \sim \pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{t=1}^T \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(c_t \mid s_t)} \right]$$

This is exactly what justifies the per-token reward used during training — the same $r_t$ formula from the Step-by-Step walkthrough below, now derived rather than just asserted:

$$r_t = -\beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{ref}(c_t \mid s_t)} \;+\; \begin{cases} r_{RM}(p,c) & \text{if } t = T \\ 0 & \text{if } t < T \end{cases}$$

The Reward Model ($RM_\psi$) does not give scores word by word. Instead, it reads the whole prompt and the finished response together just once. Then, it gives one single score ($r_{RM}$) for the entire response. Because this score is for the whole text, it cannot be split across individual words. Therefore, during step-by-step training, this final score is added only to the very last token ($t = T$). For all the words before the end ($t < T$), the reward is just the penalty for drifting too far from the baseline.

The Critic ($V_\eta$) is a separate model trained alongside the main AI. Its job is to look at each word as it is written and predict what the final score will be. This prediction serves as a baseline. By comparing the actual result to this baseline, the system calculates an “advantage” score. This score tells the system if a specific word choice was better or worse than expected. The Critic has its own separate weights and its own loss function, meaning it operates independently without sharing parameters.

3.3.2 Credit Allocation via Generalized Advantage Estimation (GAE)

Because the Reward Model's score lands only on the final token, a plain policy-gradient update would be extremely high-variance — one number at the very end would have to explain every token that came before it. The Critic $V_\eta$ addresses this by estimating the expected cumulative future reward from any given partial sequence $s_t$:

$$V_\eta(s_t) \approx \mathbb{E}_{\pi_\theta} \left[ \sum_{l=t}^T \gamma^{l-t} r_l \;\middle\vert{}\; s_t \right]$$

(In standard RLHF, the discount factor $\gamma$ is set to $1$ — there's no reason to discount future tokens within a single response.)

From this estimate, we compute a temporal-difference error at each step and use GAE to distribute credit backward across the whole sequence:

$$\delta_t^V = r_t + \gamma V_\eta(s_{t+1}) - V_\eta(s_t)$$
$$\hat{A}_t = \sum_{l=0}^{T-t} (\gamma \lambda)^l \delta_{t+l}^V$$

The resulting Advantage $\hat{A}_t$ is exactly the "was this token better or worse than expected?" signal referenced earlier — now with the precise recursion behind it, rather than just the informal description.

3.3.3 The Exact Optimization Objectives

The Step-by-Step walkthrough above writes the policy update as the plain gradient $\nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t) \cdot A_t$ for intuition — but that's not actually what makes PPO "clipped." The real objective also compares the current policy's probability against the probability under the policy that generated the sample, $\pi_{old}$, via a ratio $r_t(\theta) = \dfrac{\pi_\theta(c_t \mid s_t)}{\pi_{old}(c_t \mid s_t)}$, and then clips how far that ratio is allowed to move in a single update:

$$L^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \min\left(r_t(\theta)\hat{A}_t, \; \text{clip}(r_t(\theta), 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)\hat{A}_t\right) \right]$$

The clip is what stops a single very large advantage from causing a single very large, destabilizing weight update. The Critic, meanwhile, is trained independently with a standard mean-squared-error loss against the target return $R_t = \hat{A}_t + V_\eta(s_t)$:

$$L^{\text{MSE}}(\eta) = \hat{\mathbb{E}}_t \left[ \left( V_\eta(s_t) - R_t \right)^2 \right]$$

Real-Life Analogy of RLHF

ANALOGY

The RLHF training loop closely mirrors human mentorship and creative critique across three distinct roles: the writer and their natural habits act like the Policy and Reference Model, where an inner voice anchors the writer to prevent them from slipping into overly dramatic corporate robot speech (reward hacking); the Reward Model functions like a judge reading an entire essay from start to finish to assign a final score based on overall impact rather than interrupting with micro-grades; and finally, the Critic and GAE operate like a reflective coach who breaks down the flow of a piece to distribute credit and figure out which exact choice turned the tide.

Practical Issues of Training LLM and RLHF

While post-training and RLHF are highly effective in theory, implementing them in production reveals severe bottlenecks. Training Large Language Models (LLMs) and aligning them with human preferences involves managing complex data quality issues, engineering stability bugs, and wrestling with computational resource constraints.

The primary practical issues encountered when training LLMs and executing RLHF pipelines break down into three major categories.

1. Data and Human Feedback Bottlenecks

In post-training, data quality is the ultimate bottleneck; no amount of architectural sophistication or optimization gymnastics can compensate for noisy, misaligned, or poorly curated human preference data, making robust data engineering and alignment pipeline governance the single hardest engineering challenge in the stack.

  • The Length Bias Exploitation: In LLM optimization loop is successfully doing exactly what you programmed it to do (maximizing the reward score), while completely failing at what you actually want it to do (writing good answers). Fixing it requires constant architectural mitigation, dataset re-balancing, and meticulous hyperparameter babysitting.
  • Human Evaluator Inconsistency & Fatigue: Human annotators are not a monolith. They carry diverse cultural biases, disagree on subjective tasks, and suffer from attention decay over long sessions. Data poisoning (deliberately bad annotations) is also a constant threat.
  • The "Syndicated Plausibility" Trap: Humans struggle to accurately evaluate tasks where the AI exhibits specialized knowledge (like auditing complex code or advanced mathematics). Annotators routinely reward responses that look authoritative and correct, unintentionally training the model to become a highly convincing hallucinator.

2. Algorithmic and Training Instability

Reinforcement learning is notoriously volatile, and applying it to models with billions of parameters introduces distinct failure modes.

  • Reward Hacking: The active LLM quickly figures out how to game the system. It discovers loopholes or specific formatting patterns that maximize the score from the Reward Model without actually satisfying the true human intent.
  • Policy Collapse and Degeneration: If the RL training steps are too aggressive, the LLM suffers from "catastrophic forgetting." It can lose its baseline linguistic fluency, starting to repeat specific keywords endlessly or refusing to answer benign queries due to over-tightened safety constraints.
  • Reference Drift: To prevent policy collapse, developers use a Kullback-Leibler (KL) divergence penalty to keep the active model close to the original fine-tuned model. Balancing this penalty is a massive headache: too high, and the model doesn't learn human preferences; too low, and the model's text generation breaks down completely.

3. Computational and Hardware Challenges

The infrastructure required to execute full-scale RLHF is an engineering nightmare that frequently triggers system crashes.

How the Industry Mediates These Challenges

To bypass these issues, modern engineering teams heavily rely on two shifts:

  1. Transitioning to DPO: Moving from PPO to Direct Preference Optimization (DPO) eliminates the separate reward and value models entirely. This cuts infrastructure requirements in half and bypasses the instability of training a separate reinforcement learning agent.
  2. Adopting RLAIF: Replacing human annotators with AI Feedback (RLAIF) uses frontier models (like GPT-4 or Claude) as judges. AI judges operate instantly, do not suffer from fatigue, can handle complex logical tasks, and scale at a fraction of the cost.
