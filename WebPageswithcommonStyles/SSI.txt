<div class="content-wrapper">
    <h1>Sampling & Statistical Inference</h1>
    
    <p class="detail-description">
        Probability distributions like the <strong>Multivariate Normal Distribution</strong> allow us to describe the "shape" of data when we already know the parameters ($\mu, \Sigma$). However, in the real world, we use a small <strong>Sample</strong> to infer the truth about the entire <strong>Population</strong>.
    </p>

    <div style="text-align: center; background: #f8f9fa; padding: 20px; border-radius: 4px; margin: 15px 0; border: 1px solid #e1e8ed; font-family: 'Courier New', monospace;">
        <strong>Statistical Inference:</strong> Given a sample $X_1, X_2, \dots, X_N$ following a distribution $F$, how do we infer the properties of $F$?
    </div>

    <p class="detail-description">
        The two main branches of statistical inference are <strong>Estimation</strong> (calculating values) and <strong>Hypothesis Testing</strong> (sanity-checking claims).
    </p>
</div>

<div class="content-wrapper">
    <h2>Statistical Test of Hypothesis</h2>
    <p class="detail-description"> 
        A <strong>Statistical Test of Hypothesis</strong> is a formal "Trial by Data." We evaluate two competing statements:
    </p>

    <ul>
        <li><strong>Null Hypothesis ($H_0$):</strong> The "default" assumption of no effect. (e.g., "The mean is 65").</li>
        <li><strong>Alternative Hypothesis ($H_1$):</strong> The claim we want to prove. (e.g., "The mean is NOT 65").</li>
    </ul>

    <h3>Common Distributions for Testing</h3>
    <ul>
        <li><strong>Z-test:</strong> For large samples ($n > 30$).</li>
        <li><strong>t-test:</strong> For small samples ($n < 30$) with unknown variance.</li>
        <li><strong>$\chi^2$-test:</strong> For "Goodness of Fit" or independence.</li>
        <li><strong>F-test:</strong> For comparing variances between two groups.</li>
    </ul>

    <div class="algorithm-box">
        <span class="algorithm-title">Algorithmic Approach (6 Steps)</span>
        <ol>
            <li>Set up $H_0$ and $H_1$.</li>
            <li>Choose the appropriate test statistic ($Z, t, F, \chi^2$).</li>
            <li>Specify the <strong>Level of Significance ($\alpha$)</strong>.</li>
            <li>Calculate the <strong>Test Statistic</strong> from your sample data.</li>
            <li>Find the <strong>Table Value</strong> (Critical Value) based on $\alpha$ and Degrees of Freedom.</li>
            <li><strong>Make a Decision:</strong> If Calculated Value > Table Value, fall in the Rejection Region.</li>
        </ol>
    </div>

    <div class="highlight-box">
        <h3>The Four Possible Outcomes</h3>
        <table class="data-table">
            <thead>
                <tr>
                    <th style="background-color: #eee;"></th>
                    <th style="color: #2e7d32;">$H_0$ is True</th>
                    <th style="color: #d32f2f;">$H_0$ is False</th>
                </tr>
            </thead>
            <tbody>
                <tr>
                    <td><strong>Accept $H_0$</strong></td>
                    <td style="background-color: #f0fdf4;">Correct Decision</td>
                    <td style="background-color: #fff5f5;">Type II Error ($\beta$)</td>
                </tr>
                <tr>
                    <td><strong>Reject $H_0$</strong></td>
                    <td style="background-color: #fff5f5;">Type I Error ($\alpha$)</td>
                    <td style="background-color: #f0fdf4;">Correct Decision</td>
                </tr>
            </tbody>
        </table>
    </div>

    <h3>Step 3: Level of Significance ($\alpha$)</h3>
    <div class="intuition-box">
        <strong>The Balancing Act:</strong> As you decrease $\alpha$ (e.g., from 5% to 0.1%), you become more strict. 
        In our University example: If you raise the passing mark from 43 to 95, you rarely pass an unqualified person (Low Type I Error), but you might fail many geniuses (High Type II Error).
    </div>

    <div class="math-breakdown">
        <strong>Mathematical Definitions:</strong>
        <p>$$ \alpha = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is True}) \text{ [Type I Error]} $$</p>
        <p>$$ \beta = P(\text{Accept } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is False}) \text{ [Type II Error]} $$</p>
    </div>

    <h3>Step 5: The Critical Region ($R$)</h3>
    <div class="grid-list">
        <div class="grid-item">
            <span class="tag">Acceptance Region ($A$)</span>
            <p>If the statistic falls here, we fail to reject $H_0$. It represents the "likely" outcomes.</p>
        </div>
        <div class="grid-item">
            <span class="tag" style="background: #d32f2f;">Critical Region ($R$)</span>
            <p>If the statistic falls here, we <strong>Reject $H_0$</strong>. It represents the "extreme" outcomes.</p>
        </div>
    </div>

    <h3>Step 6: The Verdict</h3>
    <p class="detail-description">
        If your <strong>Calculated Value</strong> belongs to the set <strong>$R$</strong> (the red zone), your result is "Statistically Significant," and you reject the Null Hypothesis.
    </p>
