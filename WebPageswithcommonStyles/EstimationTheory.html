Estimation Theory and Applications

Predicting Indian elections is one of the most complex applications of estimation because the "Truth" (the actual choice of 900+ million eligible voters) is only revealed on counting day. Until then, pollsters must rely on "Snapshots" (Opinion and Exit Polls).

The Challenge: With a country as geographically and demographically diverse as India, you cannot interview every single voter (the Population). Instead, you conduct a field survey of 50,000 voters across various constituencies (the Sample) and estimate based on these samples.

In statistics, there are two widely used types of estimations:

  • (1) Point Estimate: Provides a single specific number.
    Example: "Our model predicts 285 seats."
  • (2) Interval Estimate: Provides a range of plausible values together with a confidence level.
    Example: "We are 95% confident the result is between 272 and 298 seats."

The Big Idea: Repeated Sampling

An estimate is itself a random number. Take a different sample and you get a different estimate. Suppose Party A's true vote share in a constituency is 52%, a number nobody knows until counting day. Five polling agencies each survey 1,000 randomly chosen voters and report Party A's share:

Polling agencyVoters surveyedEstimated share for Party A
Agency 11,00051.2%
Agency 21,00053.1%
Agency 31,00049.8%
Agency 41,00052.4%
Agency 51,00050.6%

Same method, five different answers. In real life you see only one of these rows, so the question is never "is my poll exactly right?" (we cannot know until counting day) but "is my polling method a good one when repeated again and again?" Every property on this page is a statement about that imagined repetition. We rarely repeat a survey for real; we either reason about it with algebra or simulate it, as in the Estimator Lab at the end of this page.

The four "good estimator" properties in the next table are four plain questions about the pile of repeated estimates.

Criteria for a Good Estimator

↻ Repeated-sampling view Each row of the table below is a statement about what the estimator does over many repeated samples, never about a single sample.
Criteria Plain Question & What Repeated Samples Show Defining Condition Real-World Example (The "Story") Key Insight
Unbiasedness Is it aimed correctly? The pile of repeated estimates is centred on the true value. $$E[\hat{\theta}] = \theta$$ The Port Depth: An unbiased sonar's repeated readings (e.g. 17.8m and 18.2m) scatter around the true 18m, so their long-run average is exactly 18m. No systematic "offset" in the tool.
Consistency Does more data help? The pile narrows onto the truth as $n$ grows. $$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\hat{\theta}_n - \theta| < \epsilon) = 1 \quad \text{for every } \epsilon > 0$$ Exit Polling: Predictions converge tightly to the truth as more voters are counted. As sample size ($n$) grows, the probability that your estimate $(\hat{\theta}_n)$ stays within a tiny distance $(\epsilon)$ of the absolute truth $(\theta)$ approaches 1 (certainty). In other words, the estimate "collapses" onto the truth as data grows.
Efficiency Is it steady? The pile is as narrow as possible (this narrowness is the variance). $$Var(\hat{\theta}) = \frac{1}{I(\theta)} \quad \left(\text{in general, } Var(\hat{\theta}) \ge \frac{1}{I(\theta)}\right)$$ Ship Tracking: Algorithm A has a variance of 5 min²; Algorithm B has 20 min². A is more efficient (relative efficiency 20/5 = 4). This signifies that the estimator has reached the 'speed limit' of precision. It is impossible for any other unbiased estimator to have a smaller variance than this. It achieves the Cramér–Rao Lower Bound, and an efficient estimator must be unbiased.
  • $Var(\hat{\theta})$: The "wobbliness" or uncertainty of our estimate. $I(\theta)$: The "Information" (Fisher information) available in the data, $I(\theta)=E\left[\left(\frac{\partial \ln L}{\partial \theta}\right)^2\right]$.
  • The Inverse Relationship: As Information ($I$) increases, the minimum possible Variance ($1/I$) decreases.
  • This specific value ($1/I$) is known as the Cramér-Rao Lower Bound (CRLB). An estimator that hits this bound is called Efficient.
Sufficiency Did we throw away anything useful? The summary keeps everything the data say about the parameter. $$f(x|\theta) = g(T(x), \theta) \cdot h(x)$$ Income Tax: To estimate the average income you only need the sum of incomes (and the head-count), not who earned what. Captures 100% of the useful information about $\theta$.
  • In the factorization formula $f(x|\theta) = g(T(x), \theta) \cdot h(x)$ (written $L_1 \cdot L_2$ below), information is "useful" only if it interacts with $\theta$.
  • The function $g$ contains both your statistic $T(x)$ and $\theta$.
  • Because $h(x)$ does not contain $\theta$, any variation in that part of the data is just "noise" that does not change your estimate of the truth.

Visualizing Bias and Variance

Example: Sample Mean is Unbiased Estimator

Context: To estimate the average height ($\mu$) of all adults in Kerala, we take a random sample of $n$ adults ($x_1, \dots, x_n$) drawn from across the state. (A sample taken only at Vizhinjam Port would not be representative and could itself be biased.)

The Derivation:

$$E[\bar{x}] = E\left[ \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} x_i \right] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^{n} E[x_i] = \frac{1}{n} (n\mu) = \mu$$

Since every observation has $E[x_i] = \mu$, linearity of expectation gives $E[\bar{x}] = \mu$ (independence is not even needed here), meaning the sample mean is "aimed" perfectly at the truth.

↻ Repeated-sampling view $E[\bar{x}]=\mu$ says: if we drew sample after sample and averaged all the $\bar{x}$ values, we would land exactly on $\mu$. In the Estimator Lab, choose "Random poll" and watch the red line settle on the dashed line.

How Many Samples Are Involved?

Two different numbers are easy to confuse:

  • Sample size ($n$): the proof above holds for any $n \ge 1$; even for $n=1$, $\bar{x}=x_1$ and $E[x_1]=\mu$. Unbiasedness does not depend on $n$. Sample size only affects the variance of $\bar{x}$, which is $\sigma^2/n$.
  • Number of samples: in a real survey you draw one sample and compute one $\bar{x}$. $E[\bar{x}]$ is the average of $\bar{x}$ over all possible samples of size $n$. It describes the long-run behaviour of the method, not something you observe in a single survey.

A small check you can do by hand. Suppose the whole population has just three heights, 150, 160 and 170 cm. Then $\mu = \dfrac{150+160+170}{3} = 160$. Draw samples of size $n=2$ with replacement. There are $3 \times 3 = 9$ equally likely samples, and each cell below shows the sample mean $\bar{x}$:

1st draw \ 2nd draw150160170
150150155160
160155160165
170160165170

The nine means add up to 1440, so $E[\bar{x}] = \dfrac{1440}{9} = 160 = \mu$. Individual samples can miss (150 or 170), but on average there is no systematic error. The same table also confirms the variance formula: the population variance is $\sigma^2 = \dfrac{(-10)^2+0^2+10^2}{3} = 66.7$, and the nine means have variance $\dfrac{300}{9} = 33.3 = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{2} = \dfrac{\sigma^2}{n}$.

But how do we know $\mu = 160$ in advance? In this toy example we don't need to estimate anything: the population is tiny and completely listed, so $\mu$ can simply be computed. That is what makes it a good classroom check, because we can compare the average of all the $\bar{x}$ values with the true answer. In a real problem $\mu$ is unknown (that is why we estimate it), and we never see all the possible samples. We rely instead on the algebraic proof above, which shows $E[\bar{x}]=\mu$ whatever the value of $\mu$ is. The table only verifies that proof on a case where the answer is known.

Likelihood Function

↻ Repeated-sampling view A contrast: the likelihood works with one observed sample, held fixed, and varies the candidate parameter. Repeated sampling is how we judge the method; the likelihood is how we use the data in hand.

The Likelihood Function $L(\theta)$ is the mathematical bridge that allows us to reverse-engineer our parameters. While "Probability" predicts the data given a fixed parameter, "Likelihood" assesses the parameter given the observed data.

  • Definition: For independent, identically distributed observations $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$, the Likelihood Function is the joint probability density (or mass function) viewed as a function of the parameter $\theta$: $$L(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} f(x_i | \theta)$$
  • The "Reverse" Logic: In our Port Depth example, if we measure 17.5m, the Likelihood Function tells us how "plausible" it is that the true depth $\theta$ is 18m versus 15m.
  • Not a Distribution: Unlike a probability distribution, the area under a Likelihood Function, $\int L(\theta)\,d\theta$, does not in general equal 1.
Reading Figure 2 step by step:
  1. The setup: a sonar reading is the true depth plus random error, with error standard deviation $\sigma = 1$ m. We took one reading and got $x = 17.5$ m. We do not know the true depth $\theta$.
  2. The question: for a candidate depth $\theta$, how probable is a reading like 17.5 m? That is the likelihood: $$L(\theta) = \frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-\theta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right)$$
  3. Candidate $\theta = 18$ m: the reading is only 0.5 m away, so $L(18) = 0.399 \times e^{-0.125} \approx 0.35$.
  4. Candidate $\theta = 15$ m: the reading is 2.5 m away (a large error), so $L(15) = 0.399 \times e^{-3.125} \approx 0.02$.
  5. Comparing: $\dfrac{L(18)}{L(15)} = e^{3} \approx 20$. The reading we actually saw is about 20 times more likely if the depth were 18 m than if it were 15 m. So 18 m explains the data much better than 15 m. This does not say the depth is 18 m, and it is not yet the probability that the depth is 18 m.
  6. The best candidate: the curve is highest at $\theta = 17.5$, the value that makes the observed reading most likely. This is the idea behind the maximum likelihood estimate.
Finding a Sufficient Statistic:

To identify a Sufficient Statistic $T(x)$, we use the Fisher-Neyman Factorization Theorem. This requires us to write the Likelihood Function as a product of two distinct functions, $L_1$ and $L_2$ (written $g$ and $h$ in the table above):

$$L(\theta) = L_1(T(x), \theta) \cdot L_2(x)$$

  • $L_1$ (The Kernel): Contains both the statistic $T(x)$ and the parameter $\theta$. This is the part of the data that "interacts" with the parameter.
  • $L_2$ (The Noise): Depends only on the sample data $x$ and not on $\theta$. This represents information that is irrelevant for estimating the parameter.

If you can successfully factor the Likelihood this way, $T(x)$ is guaranteed to be a sufficient statistic—capturing every ounce of useful information the sample has to offer.

Example: Sample Mean is a Consistent Estimator

↻ Repeated-sampling view Repeat the whole survey with $n=4$, then $n=16$, then $n=64$. Consistency says the pile of repeated estimates squeezes onto $\mu$ as $n$ grows (slide the number of voters polled in the Estimator Lab).

We want to show that the mean of a sample ($\bar{x}$) taken from a normal population is a Consistent Estimator of the population mean ($\mu$).

The Mathematical Derivation

Step 1: Properties of the Sample Mean

Given independent observations from a normal population, each with $E(x_i) = \mu$ and $V(x_i) = \sigma^2$ (independence is what gives the variance formula below):

  • Expected Value: $E(\bar{x}) = \mu$
  • Variance: $V(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$

Step 2: Chebyshev's Inequality

We use Chebyshev's Inequality to bound the probability that our estimator deviates from $\mu$ by more than a small value $\epsilon$:

$$P(|\bar{x} - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{V(\bar{x})}{\epsilon^2}$$

Step 3: Substitution and Limit

Substituting $V(\bar{x}) = \frac{\sigma^2}{n}$ into the inequality:

$$P(|\bar{x} - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \frac{\sigma^2}{n \epsilon^2}$$

Now, we take the limit as the sample size $n$ approaches infinity:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{x} - \mu| \ge \epsilon) \le \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{n \epsilon^2} = 0$$

A probability cannot be negative, so the left-hand side is squeezed to 0. Equivalently, $\lim_{n \to \infty} P(|\bar{x} - \mu| < \epsilon) = 1$, which is exactly the definition of consistency in the table above.

As $n$ increases, the probability of the sample mean ($\bar{x}$) differing from the true mean ($\mu$) by more than any fixed $\epsilon > 0$ vanishes. This mathematically proves that $\bar{x}$ is a consistent estimator of $\mu$.

Example: Efficient Estimator

↻ Repeated-sampling view $V(t_1)$ is the spread of $t_1$ over repeated samples. It is not computed from the three numbers you observed. It comes from the coefficients: for independent $x_i$, $V(ax_1+bx_2+cx_3)=(a^2+b^2+c^2)\sigma^2$, so $V(t_1)=(3^2+2^2+(-4)^2)\sigma^2=29\sigma^2$ and $V(t_2)=(3^2+(-5)^2+3^2)\sigma^2=43\sigma^2$. The symbol $\sigma^2$ (the variance of one reading) cancels in the ratio $43/29$.
The Problem:

If $x_1, x_2, x_3$ are three independent observations drawn from a population with mean = $\mu$ and variance = $\sigma^2$, find the Relative Efficiency of $t_1$ and $t_2$. Which is more efficient?

  • $t_1 = 3x_1 + 2x_2 - 4x_3$
  • $t_2 = 3x_1 - 5x_2 + 3x_3$

Step 1: Check for Unbiasedness

For an estimator to be compared fairly, we check if $E(t) = \mu$. Since $E(x_1) = E(x_2) = E(x_3) = \mu$:

  • $E(t_1) = E[3x_1 + 2x_2 - 4x_3] = 3\mu + 2\mu - 4\mu = \mu$ (Unbiased)
  • $E(t_2) = E[3x_1 - 5x_2 + 3x_3] = 3\mu - 5\mu + 3\mu = \mu$ (Unbiased)

Step 2: Calculate Variances

Since the observations are independent, $V(ax + by) = a^2V(x) + b^2V(y)$. Given $V(x_i) = \sigma^2$:

For $t_1$:

$$V(t_1) = 3^2\sigma^2 + 2^2\sigma^2 + (-4)^2\sigma^2 = (9 + 4 + 16)\sigma^2 = 29\sigma^2$$

For $t_2$:

$$V(t_2) = 3^2\sigma^2 + (-5)^2\sigma^2 + 3^2\sigma^2 = (9 + 25 + 9)\sigma^2 = 43\sigma^2$$

Step 3: Determine Relative Efficiency

Relative Efficiency (RE) is the ratio of the variances. We calculate $RE(t_1, t_2)$ as:

$$RE(t_1, t_2) = \frac{V(t_2)}{V(t_1)} = \frac{43\sigma^2}{29\sigma^2} \approx 1.48$$

An estimator is more efficient if it has a lower variance. Since $V(t_1) = 29\sigma^2$ and $V(t_2) = 43\sigma^2$, $t_1$ is more efficient than $t_2$.

The relative efficiency of 1.48 indicates that $t_1$ is roughly 48% more efficient than $t_2$.

Example: Sufficiency of the Sample Mean

↻ Repeated-sampling view Two different samples with the same $\bar{x}$ lead to exactly the same likelihood curve for $\mu$ (up to a factor that does not involve $\mu$). So once $\bar{x}$ is known, nothing else in the sample helps in estimating $\mu$.

Statement: If $\bar{x}$ is the mean of a random sample drawn from a population following $N(\mu, \sigma^2)$, show that $\bar{x}$ is a sufficient estimator of $\mu$ when $\sigma$ is known.

The Goal: We must factor the Likelihood Function $L(\mu)$ into two parts: $L(\mu) = L_1(\bar{x}, \mu) \cdot L_2(x_1, \dots, x_n)$. If $L_1$ depends on the data only through $\bar{x}$ and $L_2$ does not involve $\mu$, then $\bar{x}$ is sufficient.

The Step-by-Step Proof

Step 1: Write the Likelihood Function

For a sample from a Normal distribution, the joint PDF (Likelihood) is:

$$L(\mu) = \left( \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \right)^n \exp \left[ -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{i=1}^{n} (x_i - \mu)^2 \right]$$

Step 2: Use the Algebraic Identity

We use the standard identity for the sum of squares by adding and subtracting $\bar{x}$ inside the summation:

$$\sum (x_i - \mu)^2 = \sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2 + n(\bar{x} - \mu)^2$$

Step 3: Factor the Likelihood

Substituting this identity back into our Likelihood equation, we get:

$$L(\mu) = \underbrace{\exp \left[ -\frac{n(\bar{x} - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2} \right]}_{L_1(\bar{x}, \mu)} \cdot \underbrace{\left( \frac{1}{2\pi\sigma^2} \right)^{n/2} \exp \left[ -\frac{\sum (x_i - \bar{x})^2}{2\sigma^2} \right]}_{L_2(x_1, \dots, x_n)}$$

Conclusion

  • $L_1(\bar{x}, \mu)$: This part contains the parameter $\mu$ and the statistic $\bar{x}$.
  • $L_2(x_1, \dots, x_n)$: Since $\sigma$ is known, this part does not depend on $\mu$. It is purely a function of the sample data.

According to the Factorization Theorem, because the Likelihood can be split such that $\mu$ only interacts with the data through $\bar{x}$, we conclude that $\bar{x}$ is a sufficient estimator of $\mu$.

Key Insight for Students: This means if you know the sample mean ($\bar{x}$), keeping the individual $x_i$ measurements (the raw heights of every person in the sample) provides no additional information about the true population mean ($\mu$). The mean $\bar{x}$ has "exhausted" the information in the sample.

More on Interval Estimation

↻ Repeated-sampling view "95% confidence" is a repeated-sampling statement: build an interval from each of many samples and about 95% of those intervals will contain the true $\mu$ (see Figure 5).

The technique of interval estimation consists in the determination of two constants $a$ and $b$ such that:

$$P[a < \theta < b \text{, for a given value of } t] = 1 - \alpha$$

where $\alpha$ is the level of Significance. The interval $(a, b)$ within which the unknown value of the parameter $\theta$ is expected to lie is known as the Confidence Interval. The two constants $a$ and $b$ are confidence limits.

For example:

When $\alpha = 0.05$ and $1 - \alpha = 0.95$. Let $t$ be the sample statistic used for estimating the population parameter $\theta$. Then we have to find constants $a$ and $b$ such that:

$$P[a < \theta < b] = 0.95$$

When we are dealing with a Normal Distribution and the population standard deviation ($\sigma$) is known, the Confidence Interval estimate of $\mu$ is given by:

$$\left( \bar{x} - z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}, \bar{x} + z_{\alpha/2} \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \right)$$

Breaking down the components:
  • $\bar{x}$: The sample mean (our point estimator).
  • $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$: The Standard Error, which represents the standard deviation of the sampling distribution.
  • $z_{\alpha/2}$: The critical value from the standard normal distribution leaving an area of $\alpha/2$ in each tail. For $\alpha = 0.05$, $z_{0.025} = 1.96$, which gives the familiar 95% interval $\bar{x} \pm 1.96\,\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
Interpretation: "95% confidence" describes the method: if we repeated the sampling many times, about 95% of the intervals built this way would contain the true $\mu$. It does not mean that $\mu$ has a 95% chance of lying in one particular, already-computed interval ($\mu$ is fixed; the interval is what varies from sample to sample).

Conclusion: Estimation in Plain Words

Suppose a fruit seller wants to know the average weight of the mangoes in a truckload and weighs a handful to find out. Most people judge such an estimate by the four criteria without naming them. They ask first whether the guess is aimed correctly: a scale that reads 20 g too heavy is wrong in the same direction every time, and weighing more mangoes will not fix it (unbiased). They ask whether it is steady: two honest scales may both be right on average, but the one that wobbles by 1 g is preferred to the one that wobbles by 20 g (efficient). They ask whether more effort helps: weighing 100 mangoes should land closer to the truth than weighing 5 (consistent). And they ask whether the summary throws away anything useful: to get an average, the total weight and the number of mangoes are enough, and the order in which the mangoes were weighed adds nothing (sufficient). Estimation theory is these four everyday questions, asked about the method rather than about one handful, and made precise with mathematics.

A note on normality: The ideas of estimation (bias, variance, consistency, sufficiency, likelihood) apply to any population. They do not require it to be normal. For example, the sample mean is unbiased for any population with a finite mean, it is consistent by the Law of Large Numbers, and for large $n$ it is approximately normally distributed whatever the population looks like (Central Limit Theorem). Normality matters for the exact small-sample results on this page, such as the exact confidence interval and the fact that $\bar{x}$ is the most efficient and sufficient estimator of $\mu$. For other populations these results hold only approximately, and a different estimator may be better. For a double-exponential (Laplace) population the median beats the mean, and for a very heavy-tailed population such as the Cauchy the sample mean does not even converge.
